    Sunnyvale School District 
    Bond Measure G 
     
     
    Measure G is a $96 million school 
    bond that will be used to continue 
    the renovation and modernization 
    of all  district schools, providing 
    students and teachers with comparable classrooms 
    and facilities.  
     
    The bond will help provide schools with long term 
    facility maintenance funds and updated instructional 
    technology. The average yearly cost to property 
    owners in the Sunnyvale School District will be $15 
    per $100,000 of assessed valuation (not market 
    value). 
     
    Four schools in Sunnyvale still need to be  
    modernized: Bishop Elementary, Cherry Chase  
    Elementary, Ellis Elementary and Sunnyvale Middle 
    School. They will be  renovated to provide  
    up-to-date classrooms, libraries and computer labs. 
     
    The bond will provide funds to: 
     
     
    Renovate and upgrade older classrooms. 
     
    Update and rebuild libraries and computer labs. 
     
    Expand school offices and student support ser-
    vice areas. 
     
    Add and renovate restrooms. 
     
    Upgrade schools through the use of clean and 
    efficient “Green” energy systems.  
     
    Improve instructional technology. 
     
    The measure provides for strict accountability as 
    bond funds are spent. A committee of local resi-
    dents will be tasked with ensuring that all bond 
    funds are spent as planned for the benefit of local 
    students.   
     
    None of the money will be used for school  
    administrator or teacher salaries.  State lottery  
    monies cannot be used to fund these school  
    projects.  
     
    For more information about the Sunnyvale 
    School District and the bond measure, please 
    visit the District’s web site at www.sesd.org.

    Moving Elections to Even-numbered Years 
     
    Sunnyvale has held elections on odd numbered years 
    for as long as I can remember. In the past, many cities 
    and school districts in Santa Clara County did so as 
    well.  Every year was an election year, with the even 
    years focusing on State and Federal races and odd 
    years focusing on local issues and candidates. 
     
    This even/odd year arrangement was originally designed 
    to help keep local elections from being overshadowed 
    by the State and Federal ones. In even years voters are 
    inundated with political messages, television  
    commercials and junk mail from well-funded  
    professional campaign organizations supporting partisan 
    issues and candidates. In odd years the candidates and 
    measures are not generally using the same kind of  
    sophisticated tools and techniques. There is a better 
    chance that a message printed on plain paper using a 
    copy machine and distributed by volunteers has a 
    chance of getting through to the voter. 
     
    So why change things now? The answer is money. The 
    costs of elections are going up. Ballots have to be  
    printed.  Systems to count them have to be set up and 
    maintained. An army of paid poll workers has to be  
    recruited and trained. Each election costs the County 
    hundreds of thousands of dollars to conduct.   
     
    To pay for it all, the County divides the bill among the 
    jurisdictions involved. If all 15 cities in the County hold 
    an election, the cost is split 15 ways. If only 4 cities hold 
    an election on the same day, the cost is split only 4 
    ways. It is cheaper for a city to have an election at the 
    same time that other elections are taking place because 
    the city can benefit from the cost sharing that is the  
    result. 
     
    Over time, to save costs, cities and school districts have 
    switched from “odd” to “even” years. This makes it more 
    expensive for the odd year elections. Sunnyvale School 
    District recently switched to even year elections.  
    The City of Sunnyvale, Stanislaus school district and  
    Orchard school district are the only participants this  
    November.   
     
    Sunnyvale is now the only city in Santa Clara County that 
    will hold an election this year. With so few participants to 
    share costs with, the bill from the Registrar of Voters will 
    be stiff indeed. 
     
    Changing our elections so that they will happen on even 
    years would result in “6 figure” savings for the city. That is 
    money that can be used to pay for park maintenance,  
    Public Safety officers and library books. 
     
    The side effects of switching have to do with the kinds of 
    campaigns that would be run and the kinds of people who 
    would be likely to turn out and vote. 
     
    Turnouts in our odd year elections tend to be very low.  I 
    was elected twice to the City Council with only about 21% 
    of the eligible voters actually bothering to cast ballots.   
    Using voter history information obtained from the Registrar 
    of Voters office it is possible to figure out who that 21% is 
    likely to be. That meant I could focus my campaign  
    resources on just those people. 
     
    There are about 55,000 voters in Sunnyvale. A typical  
    campaign will have to send out at least 2 mailings, so the 
    cost of a mailing campaign in Sunnyvale could be as much 
    at $50,000. 
     
    Having an election with a low expected turnout reduces 
    these costs tremendously. I won election in 1997 by  
    aiming only at likely voters. I spent only $16,000 on the 
    campaign (which was the money Phyllis and I had saved 
    up to remodel her kitchen). I won by getting 9,000 votes 
    out of only about 12,000 cast. By “self-financing” my  
    campaign I was not very beholden to any “special  
    interests”. The low turnout election made it possible for me 
    to get elected in the way I did. 
     
    What will It be like to run for office in Sunnyvale during an 
    even year election? The short answer is “more expensive”.  
    Turnout will be higher, so there will be more mail to send 
    out. That means that more money will be needed, and it 
    will have to come from somewhere. The candidates will 
    have to do more fundraising.

    None of this is new in politics, but it 
    will be new to Sunnyvale politics.   
     
    Some could argue that a higher voter turnout would 
    mean that the results of an election would better  
    reflect the actual will of the community. Others argue 
    that moving to an even year election might help  
    moderate voices win out over extremists, because it 
    would be less likely that a small group of angry peo-
    ple could end up being a majority of actual voters on 
    election day. I believe there might be some validity to 
    that notion. 
     
    So to summarize, moving elections in Sunnyvale to 
    even years would make them cheaper for the city but 
    more expensive for candidates. That would tend to 
    favor “establishment” candidates with access to  
    funding and professional resources and would put 
    “grass roots” candidates at a disadvantage.  It would 
    also tend to make the voting electorate more  
    moderate on the whole and less likely to be skewed 
    by small but passionate extremist groups. 
     
    Hotel Tax Increase 
     
    Sunnyvale, like most cities in California, 
    imposes a local sales tax on hotel rooms 
    called the “Transient Occupancy Tax”. 
     
    If someone from New York stays in a 
    Sunnyvale hotel and has a medical emergency, they 
    don't call the New York fire department for help. They 
    call Sunnyvale 911 and get help from the Sunnyvale 
    Department of Public Safety. Hotel residents can  
    enjoy Sunnyvale parks during their visit, make use of 
    Sunnyvale streets, and can take advantage of the 
    same amenities and services that are provided to  
    regular Sunnyvale residents.  Since they don't live 
    here and don't pay the normal taxes that  
    residents pay, hotel guests pay a tax based on the 
    cost of their hotel rooms. The measure on the  
    ballot proposes an increase in the tax from 9.5% to 
    10.5%. 
     
    There is another aspect of the hotel tax that makes it 
    very attractive as a means to help pay for city ser-
    vices.  All of the other major taxes collected in 
    Sunnyvale have been “consolidated” at the state lev-
    el.  Sales tax, property tax, the vehicle license fee, 
    and all the other taxes are sent to the State of Califor-
    nia and then distributed to cities and counties from 
    there. Only the hotel tax is collected by Sunnyvale 
    and never sent anywhere else. 
     
    Why does this matter?  It matters because  
    sometimes the State of California collects tax money 
    and doesn't give it back to cities and counties.   
    During my years on the City Council there were a 
    number of tricks and gimmicks played by the state 
    government to redirect sales and property taxes away 
    from cities and towards “State” priorities.  Some of 
    those tricks were successful and cities suffered as a 
    result. 
     
    As Mary Bradley, former Finance Director for the City 
    of Sunnyvale once told me, “The [hotel tax] is the 
    sweetest and best for Sunnyvale because we don't 
    have to share it with anybody!”  It is the one tax that 
    is completely under Sunnyvale's control and that  
    cannot be confiscated by the State of California. 
     
    The people that might possibly be hurt by an increase 
    in the hotel tax are the owners of Sunnyvale's hotels.  
    Will a 1% increase in the tax rate drive hotel  
    customers away?  Probably not. The fact is that 
    Sunnyvale's current Occupancy tax is below average.  
    Raising it 1% would put it in line with the tax rate 
    charged by other communities. 
     
    Gun Safety Regulations 
     
    The third ballot measure is one 
    will create new law in Sunnyvale if 
    is it adopted.  The law will impose 
    four new requirements: that thefts 
    or losses of firearms be reported within 48 hours, that 
    firearms be stored safely, that ammunition magazines 
    be limited to a capacity of no more than 10 rounds 
    and that sales of ammunition be tracked in 
    Sunnyvale. There will be penalties for those who fail 
    to comply with these requirements. 
     
    Would this new law mean that Sunnyvale Public 
    Safety officers will be knocking on people's doors to 
    check their gun safes or count the bullets they have 
    on hand?  Of course not. Law enforcement officers in 
    California need to have probable cause, meaning 
    they have provable reason to believe some crime or 
    misdemeanor has taken place. No police officer will 
    check your house to make sure you have trigger 
    locks for your pistols.  f, however (as has happened), 
    your 3 year old grandson gets hold of your pistol and 
    shoots himself because it had no trigger lock, his 
    death would be treated as a crime and not just an 
    accident, for which you would be responsible. 
     
    The pros and cons of each part of this law have been 
    debated hotly all year in the national media so I won't 
    go into them in detail here.   
     
    There is one aspect of this that deserves some  
    examination, though. Why should this be a city  
    ordinance at all?  Why shouldn't this just be left to the 
    state government?  
     
    It is true that an ordinance in Sunnyvale wouldn't  
    cover much territory.  
     
    If ammunition sales are tracked in Sunnyvale, would-
    n't the “bad guys” just go buy their bullets in Mountain 
    View instead?

    California has some of the strictest gun 
    laws in the nation, so why does Sunnyvale need its own  
    ordinance? 
     
    I have some personal experience with what can happen 
    when state and local laws overlap.   
     
    Back in the 1990s, Sunnyvale had its own campaign con-
    tribution ordinance limiting the amount that individual do-
    nors could give and other restrictions designed to make 
    sure Sunnyvale's elections were open and honest. 
     
    In the mid-90s, a state proposition was passed that pro-
    vided greater campaign restrictions on a statewide basis.  
    Sunnyvale repealed its own ordinance since it initiative 
    no longer necessary. A few years later the state initiative 
    was found to be unconstitutional by the California Su-
    preme Court and was struck down. 
     
    As a result, Sunnyvale now has NO campaign contribu-
    tion limits.  Sunnyvale chose to rely on state law, but 
    state law changed.  Sunnyvale started out with its own 
    regulations, but now it has no regulations at all. 
     
    State level gun safety laws might make Sunnyvale's own 
    ordinance redundant, but only as long as state laws don't 
    change. 
    I was born in rural Georgia, where nature hasn't quite 
    been tamed yet.  A walk in the woods can be nice but it 
    risks encountering puma, black bear, alligator, and five of 
    the six deadly varieties of snake that exist in North Ameri-
    ca.  Firearms there are a means of providing safety to 
    one’s self and to loved ones in the face of the dark side of 
    nature.  In places like rural Georgia (or Alaska, or Idaho) 
    it is common for people to walk into banks and stores 
    carrying loaded weapons.  They are useful things to have 
    in a wild environment, and good people expect each oth-
    er to handle them responsibly. 
     
    There are no puma or black bear in Sunnyvale.  There is 
    no “wild side” of nature here.  There are only the pres-
    sures of urban life with its stresses and its rat race.  Sili-
    con Valley can be a tough place to live.  Pressure and 
    stress and guns can be a deadly combination.  In rural 
    Georgia bullets are fired at wild life.  In Sunnyvale, when 
    bullets are fired they are fired at people.  The norms of 
    gun behavior in rural areas do not apply here.  It is silly to 
    think that the same laws should apply here as well.  
     
    In this article I have attempted to explain each measure 
    as best I can. My opinion on each measure is solely my 
    own and does not reflect any official stance of the LVNA 
    or even this newsletter. If you have a different opinion 
    that you wish to express, please feel free to submit it for 
    publication in the November issue of the Villager.

    Advanced Payment Scam 
     
    By Lt. Tracy Hern, Sunnyvale Department 
    of Public Safety—Crime Prevention Unit 
     
    I wanted to pass along reminder information 
    about scams and frauds that are targeting our 
    community, and in  
    particular senior adults.  
     
    Recently, I spoke with a Sunnyvale resident who was  
    contacted about winning a Clearing House Sweepstakes. 
    The man on the other end of the phone sounded very  
    convincing, but she knew it sounded too good to be 
    true. This man requested she wire $1400 for “insurance” 
    purposes so that her winnings could be transferred to 
    her. Our resident was very skeptical so the caller eased her 
    concerns and told her someone from law enforcement would 
    call her.   
     
    The next caller identified himself as an FBI agent and told 
    her that he reviewed the documents and this was  
    legitimate. He congratulated her and then told her to send 
    the $1400. Our smart resident was still skeptical, but it was 
    now sounding possible. The new caller offered her peace of 
    mind by telling her that he would contact the Sunnyvale Po-
    lice Department and provide them with the information. He 
    told her that a police officer would then call her. About 20 
    minutes later she received another call from someone who 
    claimed to be a police lieutenant with Sunnyvale Police. He 
    assured her that this was not a scam and it was ok to send 
    the money. 
     
    Learning point- In this particular scam, the third caller 
    claimed to be a Sunnyvale police officer and in fact gave the 
    full name of a current Sunnyvale police lieutenant.  Further, 
    the number that showed on her caller ID was  
    730-7100 which appeared to be our main information 
    line.  When our resident checked and saw that the number 
    was truly associated with the Department of Public Safety 
    she was really thinking that this was a possibility.  Luckily, 
    she told the caller that she would meet with them on Monday 
    at the police station here in Sunnyvale to provide the money 
    and finish the transaction.  As you can imagine, they never 
    called back or showed up. 
    This is a CONFIRMED scam known as an “advanced pay-
    ment” scam.  As a reminder, no government agency will 
    contact you by phone suggesting, asking or telling you that it 
    is ok to send money anywhere.  Unless a celebrity shows up 
    on your door step, you did not win a clearing house sweep-
    stakes, sorry.  If it sounds too good to be true, then it proba-
    bly is a scam.  If anyone asks you to send money first to get 
    more money later, then it is a scam.  Never wire transfer 
    money to anyone you don’t personally know and then if you 
    do know them, only if you personally talk with them.  Some 
    scammers will infiltrate email and pose as a friend stuck 
    somewhere needing money. 
     
    Additional information regarding these scams and other In-
    ternet Fraud safety tips can be found on the FBI website:  
    www.fbi.gov  
    Thank you for your continued support of the City of 
    Sunnyvale, Department of Public Safety.

    Halloween Tips 
      
    Halloween is an exciting time of year for 
    kids, and to help ensure they have a 
    safe holiday, here are some tips from the 
    American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP).  
      
    ALL DRESSED UP: 
      
    Plan costumes that are bright and reflective. Make sure 
    that shoes fit well and costumes are short enough to 
    prevent tripping, entanglement or contact with flame.  
      
    When shopping for costumes, wigs and accessories look 
    for a label that clearly indicating they are flame resistant.  
      
    Consider adding reflective tape or striping to costumes 
    and Trick-or-Treat bags for greater visibility.  
      
    Because masks can limit or block eyesight, consider non-
    toxic makeup and decorative hats.  
      
    Have flashlights with fresh batteries for all children and 
    their escorts.  
      
    ON THE TRICK-OR-TREAT TRAIL: 
      
    A parent or responsible adult should always 
    accompany young children on their neighborhood 
    rounds.  
      
    Only go to homes with a porch light on and never enter a 
    home or car for a treat! 
     Remain on well-lit streets and always use the sidewalk. 
    Pedestrian injuries are the most common injuries to 
    children on Halloween.  
      
    Never cross between parked cars or out driveways. 
    Stay in a group  
      
    Carry a cell phone for quick communication.   
      
    Don't assume the right of way. Motorists may have 
    trouble seeing Trick-or-Treaters. Just because one car 
    stops, doesn't mean others will! 
      
      
    HEALTHY HALLOWEEN: 
      
    A good meal prior to parties and trick-or-treating will 
    discourage youngsters from filling up on Halloween 
    treats. 
      
    Consider purchasing non-food treats for those who visit 
    your home, such as coloring books, small toys, stickers, 
    or pens and pencils.  
      
    Wait until children are home to sort and check treats. 
    Though tampering is rare, a responsible adult should 
    closely examine all treats and throw away any spoiled, 
    unwrapped or suspicious items. 
