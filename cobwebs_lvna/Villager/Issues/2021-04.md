    MESSAGE FROM THE PRESIDENT
    Hi LVNA Neighbors,
    With the new board just elected, I wanted to 
    take the opportunity to introduce myself as 
    the new president of LVNA and share my view 
    on this role.
    I moved to the neighborhood in Jan 2016 
    with my (now) husband. My husband and 
    I love this neighborhood, in terms of the 
    people, the amenities (the parks), proximity 
    to highways and bike paths, plus great food 
    options nearby!
    When we first moved here I really enjoyed 
    reading the Villager each month, to feel 
    connected with the neighborhood, and I was 
    so excited when I received the flyer for the 
    summer LVNA picnic at Lakewood Park. We 
    attended and got to meet some folks from 
    the neighborhood; we were even one of the 
    winners of the raffle and won 2 movie tickets! It was this picnic that made me want to get more involved. After 
    2017 passed without a picnic I began asking about whether the picnic would be held in 2018 and ended up 
    planning it for that year. It was such a warm welcome to the neighborhood for me that I wanted others to have 
    that same opportunity, not to mention a chance for me to meet more of my neighbors. And I guess that has led 
    me here today, as president.
    My interest as president of LVNA is mainly driven by the idea of helping neighbors get to know one another. 
    There are several regular events throughout the year that I hope we can begin planning again, although likely 
    not until later this year. I’m also happy to help with concerns from the community, although please be aware 
    that the board (including myself) is made up of volunteers who all have their own families, jobs, and other 
    commitments. The board is encouraged to participate in the areas that they are interested in, but to not burn 
    themselves out. Unfortunately, I do anticipate areas that we will not be able to cover as there are many different 
    concerns brought to our attention, but the good news is that we are also always looking for volunteers to help. 
    If you are passionate about a particular issue that we don’t have the bandwidth to follow-up on, we are happy 
    to put you in touch with the appropriate people so you can take next steps on any concern you have about our 
    neighborhood. If you’re inclined to participate even further, we’re also always looking for new members to join 
    the board. Most of us are on the board because we’re interested in helping the neighborhood, but also mainly 
    because “nobody else wants to do it.” If we have any strong-willed neighbors who really want to take charge, 
    most of us on the board (including myself) are happy to pass the torch to anyone who would like to have a go at 
    it. Feel free to ask us more about our roles and volunteer positions, or simply enjoy our events and what we’re 
    able to offer. :)
    I look forward to getting to know more of my neighbors.
    Sincerely,
    Lydia Fotiades
    
    FACTOID
    Hopefully you’ve had a chance to read about radon in last month’s Factoid. If not, we suggest you start there for 
    an intro to what radon is and how to measure the level in your home.
    Let’s say you’ve made some measurements and discovered that your home has elevated radon levels (over 2 
    pCi/L); now what? Well, the good news is that there are methods to bring your number down. Since every home 
    is unique and we are not licensed to provide advice on what you should do, we suggest consulting with a radon 
    mitigation specialist to assess your situation and provide recommendations tailored to your requirements.
    The most common form of mitigation is sub-slab suction. If you consider yourself pretty handy and are in 
    the mood for a DIY project, this is absolutely achievable and you can save quite a bit of money. In total, the 
    component costs will be around $300-500. Hiring someone to do the work for you will likely run upwards of 
    $2,000. However, if you don’t already have the tools and aren’t able to borrow from a family member or friend, 
    you’ll likely need to rent a couple of them to speed up the job, adding to the cost.
    Most of the Lakewood Village homes still have their original 1950s slab. Also, most of the properties were built 
    with very similar construction materials and techniques. We just installed a sub-slab suction system in January so 
    there’s a good chance that some of our experiences will directly apply to your situation. If you decide to hire a 
    professional or do it yourself, keep some of these tips in mind while planning your project.
    The goal with sub-slab suctioning is to create a vacuum under your house, pulling air and radon from the area 
    under the slab into a pipe, then venting it out the roof. There are a few key requirements to keep in mind:
    •	
    For aesthetic reasons, it’s ideal to pick a location where the pipe won’t be visible.
    •	
    A combination of factors will impact the suction abilities (fan power, soil density, pipe diameter, etc.) so 
    it’s best to either pick a central point within the home or, alternatively, install multiple pipes/systems with 
    some distance between them to ensure sufficient suction from all areas under the slab. This is where radon 
    mitigation specialists can come in handy.
    •	
    The fan must be located either outside (ensure you have a fan rated for outdoor use) or in an attic. In 
    the event that you have a leak on the output side of the fan, this prevents high-concentration radon-
    contaminated air from being blown directly into the living quarters.
    •	
    The roof vent should be at least 10 feet away from any potential entrances to the home, such as doors or 
    windows, to prevent the radon-concentrated air from blowing inside if they’re open.

    of your pipe. The soil under our home is extremely compact. We’ve done enough gardening that we went 
    into this project fully expecting that so, to make the digging easier, we decided from the beginning to cut a

    RECIPE CORNER
    Cold Brew
    30 gram whole coffee beans, ground coarsely
    1 CUP water
    1.	 For cold brew, you want to use the ratio of 1 cup water to 
    30 grams of coarsely ground coffee beans. In a jar, combine 
    ground coffee beans with water. Stir to combine.
    2.	 Put lid on jar and refrigerate it for a minimum of 12 hours 
    but no more than 24 hours. 
    3.	 When ready to strain, use a coffee filter or cheesecloth over 
    a fine-mesh sieve. Place it over a cup/pitcher and pour the 
    coffee/water mixture through it.
    4.	 The cold brew drink is ready to go! Place it in the fridge or 
    drink immediately. Feel free to dilute with water, if needed.
