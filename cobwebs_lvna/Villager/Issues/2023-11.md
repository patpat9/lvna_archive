    The Preferred Concept Plan for the Lakewood Park renovation was presented at the October 24 City Council meeting.
    The Council voted 6-0 (Councilmember Cisneros was absent) to approve the Preferred Concept Plan with the
    amendment to remove the artificial turf (AT) and keep the grass fields. The presentation by city staff can be found here.
    A recording of the Council meeting can be found here (that link should go to the time point 3:50:43, which is when the
    city staff presentation on Lakewood Park started).
    A petition to ban the installation of AT in Sunnyvale parks was presented with over 300 signatures - including over 100
    from Lakewood neighborhood. During public comment, the community spoke overwhelmingly against the installation of
    artificial turf at Lakewood Park. There were 44 public speakers who spoke against artificial turf that night (either in
    person or over Zoom), and 22 of those were from Lakewood. There were only 10 speakers in favor of the artificial turf.
    Councilmembers commented on the community involvement in this effort, and they specifically said that hearing
    opposition to artificial turf from so many Lakewood residents impacted their decision. Congratulations to those
    residents who signed the petition, wrote letters to council, and attended the meeting to make sure their voices were
    heard!
    While the public speakers were able to clearly articulate the dangers of AT, some commonly-believed myths regarding the
    benefits of AT were also mentioned. For example, for years it was believed that AT is more drought-friendly than grass.
    However, this is no longer true. While the amount of water needed per year may be less with AT than with grass, when
    looking at the water usage over the lifetime of the material, grass used less water. It takes over 900 gallons of water to
    manufacture one square meter of AT, and responsible use of AT requires watering the turf to keep it cooler on hot days
    (or else the fields should be closed). The chemicals in the AT also leach into our groundwater and water supply, both
    while the field is in use and once the field has been torn out and taken to a landfill. So any water supposedly “saved” by
    the use of AT ends up contaminated with an assortment of chemicals. California state legislature recognized this and
    recently passed SB 676. SB 676 is now California state law and includes the statement that “'drought-tolerant landscaping’
    shall not include the installation of synthetic grass or artificial turf.” Santa Clara Valley Water’s “Landscape Rebate
    Program” also considers AT to be a non-qualifying material and does not offer rebates for its use.
    Nathan Chan

    Lakewood Park Renevation Update (Cont.)
    There also remains confusion about the chemicals in AT. Of particular concern are the PFAS “forever”
    chemicals, which never break down and are linked to development delays, decreased immune system
    function, increased risk of certain cancers and more. Some people argue that PFAS-free AT exists, but it
    doesn’t. These people claim that newer infills (such as sand or cork) do not contain PFAS. This may be
    true, but those infills contain other things like lead, zinc, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs).
    Additionally, the infill is only one component of AT. AT also has a carpet layer (the layer with the
    “blades”) and this layer still uses PFAS to coat the individual blades of grass. At the same time, even if
    PFAS-free AT did exist, it would still be made of petroleum-based plastic. Residents clearly indicated
    that they do not want any plastic fields (with or without PFAS chemicals) to replace the grass fields at
    Lakewood Park.
    Next steps include a more detailed design. Note that the early design of the Park also includes the
    installation of substantial amounts of poured-in-place rubber (which has its own health and safety
    concerns) in the playgrounds. Stay tuned for further updates!

    The last LVNA general meeting was held Wednesday, Oct 11 at 7:00pm at the Lakewood Park building. Guest speakers
    Cindy Spencer and JoAnn Rieke from SERV (Sunnyvale Emergency Response Volunteers) presented on Personal
    Emergency Preparedness (PEP). Handouts to help you prepare for an emergency can be found here.
    Put yourself in this situation: It’s 3:00am. It’s dark out. You’re asleep. Suddenly, you are woken up by shaking from a
    magnitude 6.7 earthquake. What do you do? (hint: the answer is not “panic”.) If the SERV message were to be
    summarized in one sentence, it would be “When disaster strikes, the time to prepare ENDS!” So the question is - what
    can you do to prepare before disaster strikes?
    First, sign up for apps that issue alerts. SERV recommends MyShake (for earthquakes), Watch Duty (for fires), and
    AlertSCC (for general Santa Clara County alerts). All of these are free to download to your smartphone.
    Second, remember that your immediate response to an emergency should be to decide if you stay or if you evacuate.
    Evacuate if your shelter is not safe (e.g., structural damage, fire, gas leak, flood). Don’t evacuate if you don’t have to
    or if the evacuation route is not safe. Regardless of if you stay or leave, plan for being self-sufficient for 3 days.

    A Communication Plan to cover how you will connect with family members if they are separated when the
    emergency happens. Make a list of important numbers (e.g., school, work, insurance agent, doctors). Make a
    second list of emergency contacts (name and number). You should have some contacts that are local and some
    that live out-of-state or are not local. In cases where local calls can’t get through, sometimes calls to people who
    are not local can go through. That not-local person can then serve as a hub for your family - everyone in your
    family calls that person, and that person can relay information to everyone in your family.
    An Evacuation Plan to cover where you will go. Make a list of evacuation sites. These should be clearly specified
    (e.g., specify front or back yard of your house). You should have some evacuation sites that are close to your
    home (e.g., neighbor’s house, local school) and farther away (so you have plans in case you have to leave your
    block, neighborhood, or city)
    Think about required transportation to get to your evacuation place: does the car have gas; have a copy of paper
    maps (or maps downloaded to your phone); make sure the route will be accessible (e.g., overpasses may fall down
    in event of an earthquake); and plan for delays in case roads get damaged or closed.
    Have a whistle with you (e.g., one for your keychain, one for your car). People can hear whistles much more
    clearly than they can hear voices.
    Have a set of emergency kits, including a medical information kit, a medical kit, a bedside bag, and a go bag.
    Details on what goes in each kit are in the link above.
    Have water! People can only go 3 days without water. Have 1 gallon per person and per pet per day (assume a
    minimum of 3 days). Do not use juice bottles or milk bottles to store water. If you store water outside, keep it out
    of direct sunlight. Keep plastic containers off cement (e.g. on a pallet or some cardboard). Do not store plastic
    water bottles in the car.

    Updates on Fremont Union High School District (FUHSD) Move From At-
    Large to By-Trustee Area Elections
    LVNA VILLAGER - NOVEMBER 2023
    PAGE 5
    The process of moving the FUHSD from at-large to trustee area elections is well underway. There has
    never been a FUHSD trustee from the 94089 zip code (and there have been very few FUHSD trustees
    from North Sunnyvale. Since Sunnyvale High School was closed in 1981, thousands of students from
    North Sunnyvale have traveled as many as 6 miles to get to our closest public high school, Fremont
    High School. Moving to trustee area elections will ensure that Sunnyvale is represented on the board
    of trustees. If we had been represented, perhaps Sunnyvale High School would not have been the
    school chosen to close in 1981. If we had been represented, perhaps students North of El Camino Real
    would have had accessible and free VTA bus passes for the past 42 years.
    The third public hearing about the FUHSD move from at-large to trustee-areas elections was held on
    Tuesday, October 17. Four preliminary maps prepared by the consultant, Cooperative Strategies, were
    presented at that meeting. To review the maps, presentations from prior meeting, and the latest
    information about this process, please visit: https://www.fuhsd.org/newsroom/trustee-areas
    It’s crucial for Lakewood residents to stay informed and engaged in this process of forming trustee
    areas. After reviewing the maps, please share your input with FUHSD by completing this Google form.  
    FUHSD has been working with the League of Women Voters Cupertino Sunnyvale to design processes
    to ensure that district mapping lines are drawn with fairness and transparency. 
    Additionally, FUHSD is forming a community-based Community Trustee Area Districting (CTAD)
    Committee. Applications for CTAD members were due October 30. CTAD will facilitate the process of
    gathering input and developing maps which will be considered by FUHSD Trustees for elections from
    2024 through 2032. CTAD will also help ensure the districts accurately reflect the needs and interests
    of the people they serve. More information about the committee is available here:
    https://www.fuhsd.org/newsroom/trustee-areas/ctad
    If you’d like to stay informed about the process and receive calendar invites to school board
    meetings, please email SunnyvaleEquityInEducation@gmail.com or go to https://bit.ly/joinSEE to join
    Sunnyvale for Equity in Education (SEE).
