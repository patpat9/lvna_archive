    entry to it and another mailbox by the front 
    door of the main house.

    paved over front yard for parking. He showed 
    me a n example of a house that he cited for 
    have too much front yard paving and how the 
    resident have now complied: Before, the front 
    yard was covered by different colored paving 
    stones, not looking to bad but not to city code; 
    however, what the resident did to  comply was 
    to throw the paving stones into a scattered 
    heap, which really made it look bad. While they 
    are complying to the code, they made the 
    situation worse. This is a situation that went 
    from bad to worse and the neighborhood pays 
    for it. 
     
    We also loo ked at junk cars and overgrown 
    landscapes.  
     Rich has a job that is tough and thankless 
    but he is good at it and we are lucky to have a 
    motivated person assigned to Lakewood. ¢

    How Far We Have Come 
    - Brian Smithson  
     
    I’ve been reading some histori cal Villager 
    newsletters and neighborhood association 
    meeting minutes (see “Lakewood Village 
    History Project” in last month’s issue), and I 
    happened upon an unusually passionate letter 
    from a Lakewood Village resident urging the 
    neighborhood association to  not bring up a 
    particular issue at an upcoming meeting. The 
    year was 1964, and the issue was Proposition 
    14. Since I was only nine years old at that 
    time, and was living in Washington State, I 
    was unfamiliar with this proposition, but this 
    letter captured  my interest and so I looked into 
    it further. Some of you may already know 
    about it, but for the rest of us, let me fill you in 
    on some California history: 
     
    I used to think that neighborhood segregation 
    was something that happened in the South, 
    but de fact o segregation was quite common in 
    California and throughout the country. For 
    example, it was not unusual to find restrictive 
    covenants on residential property deeds that 
    prohibited the owner from selling their property 
    to people of particular racial, ethni c, or 
    religious backgrounds. Many of these 
    covenants are still on the books, but they are 
    not legally enforceable. In 1959, the California 
    State Legislature enacted the Unruh Civil 
    Rights Act that made this kind of discrimination 
    illegal for public housing . In 1963, the Rumford 
    Act extended equal housing opportunity 
    beyond the realm of public housing.  
     
    However, a backlash occurred in 1964, and a 
    proposition to repeal the Rumford act was put 
    on the California ballot. This is ironic when you 
    consider that 19 64 was the year that the 
    Federal government put into law a sweeping 
    Civil Rights act. Nonetheless, Proposition 14 
    passed by a margin of over 65% statewide,  
     and it wasn’t only the traditionally 
    conservative areas of the state that voted in 
    favor of Prop osition 14 – even San Francisco 
    County voters passed the measure. It was a 
    divisive and highly charged issue at the time, 
    and few years later, the California Supreme 
    Court overturned Proposition 14 as 
    unconstitutional. Today, we have fair housing 
    and fair lending laws in place. 
     
    After reading about this, I wondered what 
    could have possessed so many Californians to 
    vote in favor of Proposition 14? As far as I can 
    determine, it was primarily the result of fear 
    and ignorance. Neighborhoods were far more 
    segregated then than they are now, and some 
    people feared that when “minorities” moved 
    into a predominantly white neighborhood, their 
    property values would decline. Unfortunately, it 
    became self -fulfilling if those fears caused the 
    white people to move out: as p eople and 
    businesses moved away, the local tax base was 
    reduced, causing a reduction in local services, 
    which in turn caused a lasting decline in the 
    neighborhood’s intrinsic value. 
     
    So what does this have to do with Lakewood 
    Village today? Our neighborhoo d enjoys 
    amazing diversity, and that diversity is one of 
    the things that I love about Lakewood Village. I 
    don’t have current statistics for Lakewood 
    Village, but the 2000 Census for Sunnyvale 
    says that the city is 32% Asian, 15% Hispanic, 
    and 11% other non -Caucasians. I suspect that 
    Lakewood Village is even more diverse than 
    that. Compare that to a 1960 Urban Renewal 
    survey which says that Lakewood Village was 
    98% white! 
     
    Did our property values decline with the growth 
    of such diversity? No, a home worth $1 5,000 in 
    1957 is likely worth $400,000 today. That’s a 
    7.6% compounded annual rate of return, easily 
    outperforming the Dow Jones Industrial and 
    S&P 500 stock indexes over the same period.  
     
    Did our diversity cause any new problems, like 
    the ones you read ab out from time to time in 
    this newsletter: junker cars, shopping carts, 
    weeds, crime, trash cans left out, vandalism 
    and graffiti, peeling paint, kids hanging out 
    around commercial establishments, etc.? No, 
    you may be surprised to know that nearly all of 
    those problems have been widely reported in 
    the Villager newsletter as early as 1959!

    As far as I can tell, the only things that have 
    truly changed in Lakewood Village are that we 
    have many more cars per person, there are 
    more residents per household (a matter of 
    economics more than ethnicity), we use a lot 
    more electricity, and instead of the mosquitoes 
    and crickets that used to plague this area, now 
    we have those annoying little ants.  
     
    My point is t his: when we look at how to solve 
    neighborhood problems, let’s look at the issues 
    head-on and not try to assign blame to one 
    group of people or another. Most of our 
    neighborhood issues have been around for 
    longer than most of us have lived here, and 
    they h ave not resulted from any particular 
    group of people joining the neighborhood. 
    When we solve these problems, we’ll do so by 
    addressing them as generic “people problems” 
    and not as specific “problem people”.  
     
    With that in mind, I’ll be out clearing up some 
    of the weeds and litter in my front yard this 
    month. Maybe I’ll see you out there! ¢

    Neighborhood Preservation: 
    A Tour of Lakewood Village 
    - Richard Gutierrez 
    Over the past month, the Neighborhood 
    Preservation Division has been circulating the 
    Lakewood Village area.  During  these visits 
    through your neighborhood, many violations 
    were noted.   Sixty courtesy notices were 
    mailed to residents informing them that their 
    trash and recycling containers were in violation 
    and were not being stored properly. 
     
    The City of Sunnyvale req uires residents to 
    store trash and recycling containers out of view 
    from the street.  Containers may be placed at 
    the curb the day before collection pick -up and 
    must be returned to a storage area out of the 
    public view by the end of the collection day.  
     
    Please be aware that if you are found in 
    violation of the SMC 19.38.030, you may be 
    issued a citation of $25.00 for each day the 
    violation exists. 
     
    This ordinance was enacted to enhance the 
    visual image of the community. 
     
    In addition to storing trash contain ers properly, 
    you can improve the appearance and safety of 
    your home and neighborhood by placing your 
    trash and recyclables in the appropriate 
    containers and keeping the container lids 
    closed.  This reduces the amount of litter and 
    debris that is blown abo ut the community and 
    helps control insects and rodents by 
    eliminating the food source. 
     
    For more information, contact: 
     
    Richard Gutierrez 
    City of Sunnyvale 
    Neighborhood Preservation Specialist 
    (408) 730-7447 ¢

    The Parrots are Back! 
    - Brian Smithson 
    On a recent Sunday morning, I was delighted 
    to see a flock of about a dozen wild parrots in 
    Lakewood Village. These parrots are mostly 
    Mitred Conures (shown in the photograph, 
    below) and they have lived at St. Martin’s on 
    Central Avenue in Sunnyvale for at least ten 
    years. There 
    is a flock of 
    approximately 
    60 parrots in 
    San Francisco 
    and there is 
    also a flock in 
    Palo Alto. 
     
    Nobody knows 
    precisely how 
    the parrots 
    came to live 
    in the Bay 
    Area, but it 
    was probably 
    not through 
    migration. 
    Conures are 
    native to 
    Mexico and 
    Central and 
    South 
    America, and 
    it is more 
    likely they 
    were lost or 
    escaped pets 
    that became 
    successful 
    living and 
    breeding in the 
    wild with our 
    mild Bay Area climate. 
     
    I don’t know much about wild birds or their 
    feeding habits, but these parrots seem to  like 
    to crunch on the buds of trees around here, 
    and I usually see and hear them at least once 
    each year in the springtime. 
     
    If you hear a lot of squawking or a strange 
    crunching sound, look up in the trees and you 
    might be delighted to see them too! ¢

    Child Safety on the Internet 
     
    ♦  Place the computer in a centrally located 
    area in your home -not in a child’s bedroom. 
    This prevents “secret” communications or 
    access.  
    ♦  Talk with your children about the Internet. 
    Explain that it is an excellent source of 
    information, but some sites ar e 
    inappropriate and they are expected to stay 
    away from these sites.  
    ♦  Establish time frames for Internet access.  
    ♦  Keep an open line of communication with 
    your children. Discuss their Internet 
    experiences and guide them to sites that 
    are age-appropriate. 
    ♦  Consider installing software that can block 
    or filter Internet sites or certain words that 
    may indicate inappropriate sites.

    rooms…  
     
    ♦  Teach him/her never to give out any 
    personal information including: name,

    larger Internet Service Providers (ISP) have 
    moderated chat rooms with appropriate

    meet in person or send photographs.

    and notify you immediately if this happens.

    Family Daycare, Part 2 
    - James and  Gerlinde Daugherty  
    Last time I wrote about family daycare I gave a 
    very basic facts -only look at family daycare 
    business.  With the limited space I have, there 
    is much to say, and so will try to give a more 
    personal view of family daycare using our 
    daycare experiences as material. 
     
    Budgeting 
    My wife and I use an Excel spreadsheet on a 
    home computer to keep track of our income, 
    expenses, and do our budgeting.  This spread 
    sheet has a column for each of our expense 
    categories plus a reserve column so we can 
    track a specific cost. These costs are averaged 
    for the year on an ongoing basis and are 
    posted next to a column with the same 
    category item from last year.  This allows us to 
    see how our expenses compare to last year.  
    We know on a weekly basis if we are going to 
    have to cut expenses or raise rates.  
     
    Our reserve column that we use for tracking a 
    specific cost is to see if the cost of this item is 
    excessive or can be eliminated some how.  One 
    item we tracked was the cost of diapers for one 
    year.  We discove red that we were spending 
    $700 per year on diapers.  
     
    Instead of raising rates we asked our parents 
    to begin bringing their own.  Everyone 
    benefited. Only the parents with diapered 
    children picked up the extra cost, thus it was 
    unnecessary to raise everyon e’s rate.  The 
    amount saved allowed us to pass the savings 
    on to other categories that were getting out of 
    budget and needed an expense increase.  
     
    To benefit our long term parents, when we see 
    that we are getting close to needing a rate 
    increase we attempt  to raise incoming parents 
    first, then our regular parents if necessary.  
    Also rate increases are scheduled for the 
    beginning of the year whenever possible. 
     
    To supplement cost controls we are also on the 
    State food program and apply for grants 
    whenever they become available. 
     
    Family Daycare means Family 
    Our parents, or clients, are very much treated 
    like an extended family to us. Even though it is 
    in our contract to charge for overtime we 
    seldom if ever do.  With the economy and 
    Silicon Valley traffic the way it is, we don’t 
    want any of our parents risking their lives 
    trying to save on overtime.  
     
    One of our parents a few years back had an 
    older child in a preschool and had to make two 
    stops for her children.  The preschool charged 
    her a dollar a minute in overtime after 6 p.m.  
    She lived in Gilroy and worked in Santa Clara.  
    She’s retired now, in her early forties, and last 
    we heard, is living on her husband’s income. 
     
    We make arts and Crafts projects with our 
    children as part of our educational program.  
    Some of our older children that have left us for 
    preschool miss having something to show mom 
    and dad like their younger brothers or sisters 
    do, so occasionally we’ll put together a kit for 
    an older sibling so the parents can work 
    together on it with them wh en they get home.   
     
    At least once a year we will put on a party, 
    rent an astrojump, and have a buffet type 
    lunch for our kids, and their parents.  Also 
    invited to these parties are children and their 
    parents who have left us, sometime years ago. 
    We occasi onally do childcare for our parents 
    that want an evening out without the kids.  
    Sometimes over night and sometimes on a 
    weekend night. We work extra time for parents 
    who get stuck in meetings or rush hour traffic.  
    This is part of the business. Ten -hour days are 
    just about a norm for us. 
     
    War Stories from the Front 
    While working in this business you get to meet 
    and talk to many people, parents, social 
    workers, and other providers.  You hear a lot of 
    stories and share happenings with each other.  
    Here are some of those stories.  
     
    When my wife first started her daycare one of 
    our clients was a single working mom who 
    obviously was quite worn down from her 
    routine.  One morning she showed up at our 
    daycare as usual, but with out her child.  Mom

    people and professionals.  They apparently 
    couldn’t agree on anything between them, not 
    even their daughter’s name. Mom called her

    time and care to our other kids.  With a lot of 
    regrets we gave mom notice.  At this time we 
    found out from the mother that we were her 
    7th daycare provider in as many months. 
     
    Gearing Down 
    My wife has been in family daycare for over 
    thirty years now, and I have be en in it with her 
    full time going on twelve years.  In a few years 
    it will be time for us to retire. This article was 
    intended for parents to better understand the 
    daycare process and as a guiding example for 
    those contemplating going into this profession.  
     
    During this time we’ve helped families raise 
    over a hundred kids, both full time and part 
    time.  We hope we’ve made a positive 
    difference to these families we call friends.  We 
    thank the LVNA newsletter, the editor and his 
    wife, and the staff for this op portunity to briefly 
    tell our story.  We hope too, that there are 
    people out there willing and able to pick up 
    where we leave off.  Its hard work, but very 
    rewarding. 
     
    Thank you for taking the time to read this 
    article. We enjoyed doing it . ¢

    A Volunteer Who Makes Lakewood 
    Village Great 
    - Craig Crawford  
    I would like to recognize neighborhood 
    volunteer Joe Levy on Manzano for his service 
    to Lakewood Village. Joe has been cleaning up 
    the empty lot at the corner of Sandia and 
    Lawrence Expressway for the past few years. I 
    was finally able to actually see him at work and 
    asked him about his great giving to the 
    community. Joe does the cleanup work on the 
    neglected corner because it needs to be done, 
    simple as that. It’s volunteers like Joe who 
    make this the best place in Sunnyvale to live. 
    In the conversation I had with Joe, he 
    expressed a desire to make Lakewood a better 
    place to live, and he is not just t alking, he is 
    out putting sweat into his statement.  Joe also 
    told me he throws the best 4 th of July party in 
    Lakewood. I haven’t been to it yet but if he 
    puts as much effort into the party as the corner 
    I have no doubt about it. Thank you Joe for 
    you giving. ¢
