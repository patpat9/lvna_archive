    A Story of Belonging: Sunnyvale High School
    Monday, November 18, 7:00pm
    Sunnyvale Heritage Park Museum, 570 E Remington Dr, Sunnyvale, CA 94087
    Author Karen V. Hansen, PhD will give insights into this multiracial working-class school in California
     in conversation with her classmate Robert Handa, reporter at NBC Bay Area News. Opened in 1956
    , Sunnyvale High School, the second public high school in Sunnyvale, followed a groundbreaki
    ng vision during a time of interracial tension in the 1960s and 70s with the desire to achi
    eve collaboration between students and educators, aiming to reduce violence and enrich the curriculum. The inclusion of US Black history and Mexican American literature as well as increasing girls’ access to sports engendered pride and persistence in the students.
    .
    In Hansen’s new book Working-class Kids and Visionary Educators in a Multiracial High School,
     Hansen lets the students and educators during the 25-year life of the school share their experiences 
    and the impact the school had on their lives.

    Matt Savage, Santa Clara Department of Public Health’s
    Substance Use Prevention Program
    Matt discussed the County program, his work in the community,
    and increasing concern about emerging products, especially for
    youth. He shared information about synthetic cannabis, a
    substance that is nearly identical to traditional cannabis but is
    sold widely at retailers outside of regulated cannabis dispensaries.
    It is not FDA-approved, and testing is not required for sale. A
    recent study found that 11% of high school students have used this
    product, and another found that it is easily obtained by youth at
    local retailers.
    Matt also noted that earlier this year, Public Health staff found
    synthetic cannabis and other harmful products for sale at multiple
    retailers in the City of Sunnyvale. Some of these retailers were
    near schools and residential neighborhoods.

    Officer Chen, Update on Neighborhood Safety
    Officer Chen provided a few quick updates. Sunnyvale Department of Public Safety (DPS) is not
    actively enforcing against illegal street vendors. A state law several years ago decriminalized street
    vending, so the City can only regulate these vendors if it is a public safety issue. He also noted that
    food trucks are permitted by a county department, must have a Sunnyvale business license, and are
    limited on where they can operate. Permits are not actively enforced, but residents with concerns
    can submit a request through Access Sunnyvale (https://sunnyvale.dynamics365portals.us/)
    DPS runs five shifts across the City. During daytime fully staffed hours, there are 2-3 officers per
    beat at a time. Officers spend most of their time going from call to call. Sunnyvale has a strong
    relationship with nearby cities, so they can call on other cities for support if needed.
    For non-emergency general questions, call Public Safety at 408-730-7110

    Update on Development at 4590 Patrick Henry Drive
    Development of the multi-unit housing complex at 4590 Patrick
    Henry Drive continued this month with an architectural review
    Development Review Hearing in Santa Clara on Wednesday, Oct
    16.
    Located just across the aqueduct bridge east of the Calabazas
    Creek Trail (and in Santa Clara), the housing development will
    replace the existing one-story light industrial building with an
    eight story, 284 unit multi-family residential building. This is part
    of Santa Clara’s Patrick Henry Drive Specific Plan (PHDSP). The
    development brings much-needed housing to the community,
    including 15% (42) affordable units. 
    The red box is the location of proposed development. The
     purple line is the greenbelt connecting to the Calabazas Cree
    k Trail. Note the proximity of the new development to t
    he aqueduct, as well as homes on Manzano Way and Oak Creek 
    Way on the east side of our neighborhood.
    While supportive of bringing high density housing to our community, Lakewood residents have raised
    privacy concerns about the height of the building. The building would be up to 86 feet tall, and there
    is a question regarding how visible backyards in the Lakewood neighborhood would be from units
    located at the top of the building. Preliminary drone footage by residents suggests that, from the top
    of the building, it would be easy to see into backyards on Manzano Way and Oak Creek Way. There
    would also be reasonable visibility from the building to Fairwood Park and the playgrounds at
    Fairwood Elementary School.
