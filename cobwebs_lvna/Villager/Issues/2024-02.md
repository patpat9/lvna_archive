    Our first general meeting of the year was on January 10 at 7:00pm at the Lakewood Park Building. Our guest
     speaker was one of our favorites - Bailey Hall, from the City’s Department of Environmental Services. She treated us to 
    us to an evening of “Talkin’ Trash: Diving Into Garbage, Recycling, and Organics.” Here are some highlights:
    Did you know that Sunnyvale doesn’t have a landfill? The Sunnyvale landfill reached capacity and closed down
     in 1993. Today, all of our trash goes to the SMaRT station, where it gets sorted. The garbage is compacted an
    d goes to the Kirby Canyon Landfill in Gilroy. 
    The SMaART station turned 30 years old in October 2023! It processes 1000 tons of material each day (which
     includes everything it gets from Sunnyvale and Mountain View). We have a 68% diversion rate, meaning that 
    68% of the material that goes to the SMaRT station gets sent somewhere besides the landfill.
    Sunnyvale’s goal is to reach a 90% diversion rate, which qualifies us to be “zero waste.”

    Not everything that can be recycled should be recycled.
     For example, technically, Styrofoam is recyclable, bu
    t the market and demand for recycled Styrofoam 
    is extremely small and unstable. Since people use 
    way more Styrofoam than the recycling market can support, 
    Styrofoam should go in the garbage.
    Less than 10% of single-use plastics get recycled
    Different cities have different methods of
     communication. San Jose tells people to thro
    w everything in bins and then it gets sorted off-site. Sunnyvale tells people what actually can and can’t get recycled. This makes it easier to sort at the SMaRT station, and it keeps us honest about where our trash actually goes. .
    Glass and metal can get recycled over and over. Paper
     can be recycled, but only a handful of times. Plastic can 
    be recycled 1-2 times before it is no longer recyclable.

    Wondering about the FoodCycle program? In 2021, 65% of Sunnyvale
     residents participated in the program, which diverts 5000 tons of foo
    d per year from the landfill. FoodCycle is going to be even mo
    re important now. Recent state law SB1383 requires communities to red
    uce the amount of organics going into the landfill by 75% by 2025. All of the 
    food that goes into the FoodCycle bin is used to generate electricity.
    Some final food for thought:
    There’s a reason the order is “reduce, reuse, recycle.” Want to reduce
     your environmental footprint? First, reduce how much you buy. Second, reuse what you buy. And only then should you recycle it .
