    July in Your Yard and 
    Garden 
    • Weed. Do not let the weeds form 
    seedheads. 
    • Add young, seedhead-free weeds to 
    the compost pile. Remember to keep 
    the pile as damp as a wrung-out 
    sponge for faster composting. 
    • Watch out for snails and earwigs, both of which 
    devour seedlings. 
    • Plant bulbs that bloom in autumn. 
    • Use a strong spray from the hose to wash away 
    aphids. 
    • With fruit crops ripening this month, watch out for 
    ants. Wrap fruit tree trunks with a sticky tape to 
    control ants (wrap low so that birds, particularly 
    hummingbirds, will not be attracted) and if 
    necessary, spray. 
    • Water plants as needed. 
    • Insulate the garden soil from the hot, dry weather by 
    applying a two-inch-thick mulch. 
    • Fertilize plants if you see signs of pale, lackluster 
    growth. Fertilize the lawn. Fertilize all flowering 
    plants that are about to bloom. Fertilize camellias. 
    Fertilize roses, except most antique roses. In really 
    hot areas, roses will probably stop flowering until the 
    weather cools. 
    • Pinch off dead flowers. Make sure you get the entire 
    flower head when deadheading annuals—particularly 
    the swollen ovaries where seeds form. 
    • Transplant summer-flowering plants, such as cosmos, 
    petunias, and zinnias that will endure heat, into the 
    bedding plot. Hot weather is hard on newly 
    transplanted plants. Shade them for a few days with 
    the temporary shelter of an umbrella or wooden 
    shake. Stake tall sprawling flowers such as dahlias. 
    • Continue to plant the vegetable garden. Thin 
    vegetable seedlings that were sown too close 
    together. Harvest vegetables as they ripen. Pick them 
    early in the day. 
    • Soak all newly transplanted plants. Prune berry vines 
    when you have finished harvesting. 
    These tips are from the book Northern California Gardening: A 
    Month-by-Month Guide, by Katherine Grace Endicott, 
    published by Chronicle Books, San Francisco, copyright 1996, 
    and are used with the publisher’s permission. ■ 
    No Hazwaste in the Dumpsters! 
    July 16 is the next Dumpster Day in Lakewood Village, 
    but don’t plan on putting paint, pesticides, solvents, 
    batteries, fluorescent bulbs, or automotive fluids (oil, 
    brake fluid) into the dumpster in your area. These items 
    are hazardous waste, so they need to go to the 
    Household Hazardous Waste Drop-Off Event Site near 
    the SMaRT Station and Recycling Drop-Off Center off 
    Caribbean Drive and Borregas Avenue (it’s a short trip 
    north on Lawrence Expressway to Caribbean and 
    Borregas). The site will be open on July 16 to take 
    these materials. 
    Donate Computer Monitors, TVs, and Laptop 
    Computers 
    Plan ahead to get rid of televisions, computer monitors, 
    and laptop computers. They cannot be put into your 
    trash or into the dumpsters on Dumpster Day, but they 
    can be donated to, for example, HOPE, which sells 
    them to an authorized state recycler. You can take them 
    to 1777 Agnew Road, Santa Clara, Monday through 
    Thursday between 8 a.m. and 6 p.m. A search on the 
    Web will lead you to other recycling options. 
    Sunnyvale residents can also take these items to the 
    SMaRT Station 7 days a week between 8 a.m. and 5 
    p.m. The SMaRT Station requires a special handling 
    fee of $15 for each item. Call (408) 730-7262 for more 
    information. ■

    June 9 General Meeting 
    Sunnyvale Department of Public Safety Chief Don 
    Johnson briefed us on the department’s role and 
    responsibilities, as well as on issues pertaining

    report suspicious activity you should call (408) 7307110 and give specific information, such as time of day 
    of the activity, physical descriptions of people acting 
    suspiciously, and license plate numbers. The drug 
    hotline number is (408) 245-DRUG (3784). (See the 
    back page of any issue of the Villager for phone 
    numbers to call about other specific complaints.)

    Pocket Bikes and Powered Scooters —
    Safety First 
    -Skip Gottschalk 
    The traffic and congested parking in the streets of 
    Lakewood Village make riding pocket bikes (minimotorcycles) and powered scooters hazardous, people 
    at the June LVNA general meeting noted. You have to 
    be a teenager to operate them, the Sunnyvale 
    Department of Public Safety reminds parents. 
    The department gave us the following information 
    about the use of pocket bikes and powered scooters: 
    • Pocket bike operators must have a valid license with 
    a motorcycle endorsement, must wear a helmet, and 
    must be at least 15-½ years old. The pocket bike 
    must have all the standard safety features (lights, 
    etc.) to be street approved. 
    • Powered scooters are not classified as motor 
    vehicles, so no license is required, but the operator 
    must wear a helmet and be at least 16 years old. If 
    ridden on a street, the scooter must be ridden in the 
    bike lane, or as close to the right-hand curb as 
    possible. 
    Parents need to make sure that their teens operate 
    pocket bikes and powered scooters safely—and that 
    younger children not be permitted to use them. 
    If you see someone underage operating one of these 
    vehicles or operating it unsafely or without required 
    safety equipment, you can call 911 and request that an 
    officer respond. ■
