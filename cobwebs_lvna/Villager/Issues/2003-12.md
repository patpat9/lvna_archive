    At the last LVNA general meeting we were 
    pleased to have Lauren Weiser, an arborist 
    from the city, come and speak to us.   First off, 
    Mr. Weiser reminded us that trees do buffer 
    the wind, filter pollution, put out oxygen, and 
    do look nice too.  But he understands they can 
    create trouble too.  A number of people had 
    previously expressed their unhappiness with 
    the city trees that grow in their public right of 
    way.  (The public right of way is defined as the 
    eleven feet from the fa ce of the curb back 
    toward your house).  Complaints generally 
    involved either being allergic to the trees or 
    being unhappy with the debris that the trees 
    are dropping.  Mr. Weiser was very 
    informative, and has been with the city long 
    enough that he knows off the top of his head 
    which trees are planted where when people 
    called out what streets they lived on.   Many 
    trees, he says, have only become problems 
    recently.   Hackberry trees, for instance, have 
    only started to drop sticky stuff all over the 
    street and sidewalks in the past few years.  
    (This is the result of aphid infestation).  Privet 
    trees were a big area of concern as many 
    people are now allergic to them.  The problem 
    has become so widely understood that the city 
    will remove city Privet trees upon request.  So 
    if you have a Privet tree and want it removed, 
    you just have to call Neighborhood 
    Preservation Division, Community Development 
    Department at 408 730-7444.  If you have 
    trouble with Holly Oaks dropping acorns, a 
    well-timed pruning in the spring or fall can help 
    reduce the crop.   Aphid infestation of the 
    Hackberry trees can be helped by properly 
    timed pesticide application.

    Two questions that were asked were: why did 
    they plant the trees th ey did, and what are 
    they planting now that they know these trees 
    are problematic?  The answers were that the 
    tree choices are based mainly on availability at 
    reasonable cost and appropriateness to the 
    climate and water use (the less the better).  
    They are no longer planting Tulip, Privet, Holly 
    Oak or Hackberry trees, all of which have been 
    identified as creating problems.  Two of the 
    trees they currently plant are Swamp Myrtle 
    and Fan West Ash.   
     
    What are we allowed to cut off our city trees?  
    Mr. Weiser said that limbs up to one inch in 
    diameter are okay for people to trim 
    themselves, but anything greater than that 
    needs to be left to the city, or you can call a 
    professional pruning service if you need or 
    want pruning done before the city has it 
    scheduled. 
     
    Overall the message from Mr. Weiser was that 
    the city does want to help and support us with 
    our tree issues, but we need to understand 
    that with budget cuts the number of staff 
    members for tree maintenance has dropped 
    26%.  (They have 4 fewer tree cutters than 
    before).  As part of the cost cutting measures, 
    there is no longer any pesticide spraying 
    provided by the city, and the trimming interval 
    has been extended to every 6-8 years.  
    However, if you have a tree that is blocking a 
    city streetlight or dragging down to car level, 
    you can call for service.  Similarly, you should 
    also call if a tree is interfering with high power 
    electricity lines.  Here are phone numbers to 
    call:

    - City press release #11 10 
     
    The City of Sunnyvale has once again been 
    ranked as one of America’s safest cities. Of the 
    350 cities ranked nationa lly in all population 
    categories, Sunnyvale was rated the fourth 
    Safest City in America in its population 
    category (100,000 – 499,999) and 10th safest 
    city overall. 
     
    The Morgan Quitno Safest City Award program 
    reviews FBI statisti cs from 350 cities 
    nationwide. The annual rankings are based on 
    six basic crime categories: murder, rape, 
    robbery, aggravated assault, burglary and 
    motor vehicle theft.  
     
    “We have always been proud of the excellent, 
    dedicated work performed by the members of 
    the Sunnyvale Department of Public Safety,” 
    said Sunnyvale Mayor John Howe. “Our annual 
    resident surveys have demonstrated that our 
    residents feel safe in their community and this 
    continuing national recognition again 
    underscores that our public safety officers are 
    among the finest in the nation.” 
      
    California cities fared well in the annual survey 
    which was released this week.  Four California 
    cities ranked in the top ten among cities of all 
    size, and in Sunnyvale’s size category, a total 
    of six California cities were ranked in the top 
    ten. 

    49 years ago: In 1954, a 
    developer 
    submitted plans for 
    a 409-home tract 
    where Unit II is 
    today, on the 
    northeast corner of Lawrence Station 
    Rd. (now Expressway) and the Bayshore 
    Hwy. (now the US-101 Freeway). Also 
    submitted were plans for a 304-room 
    Holiday Inn motel (now a Ramada Inn). 
     
    48 years ago: After 13 years of drought, some 
    ambitious cloud-seeding resulted in the 
    Christmas Flood of 1955. It rained for 
    almost two weeks and Sunnyvale’s 
    lowlands (including parts of Lakewood 
    Village) sustained some flooding. 
     
    44 years ago: In 1959, the first Lakewood 
    Village Holiday Parade was held, and 
    also that year, the first Holiday Lighting 
    Contest was held. 
     
    43 years ago: In 1960, the Lakewood Village 
    Merchant’s Association was formed.

    At the November general meeting of the LVNA 
    we were joined by Parks Superintendent Curtis 
    Black, who had some great news to share about 
    upcoming changes to Lakewood Park.  As part 
    of a project out for bid right now, several areas 
    of the park will get improvements and 
    upgrades.  The roller rink area will get a new 
    surface, and some obstacles will be added for 
    the enjoyment of skateboarders and 
    rollerbladers.  Where the old teepees used to be 
    will be a new gazebo, and a water feature will 
    be added near the park buildings and the 
    playground, with an aerospace theme of course!  
    The fire pit area may ha ve some concrete and 
    wood chips added, as well as a hose bib to help 
    extinguish fires.  If possible, there will also be a 
    volleyball pit.  The plan calls for construction to 
    be complete by late spring or early summer. 
     
    The bad news, he said, is that with city budget 
    cuts future projects will be limited in scope to 
    health and safety issues.  Luckily for us this 
    plan squeaked through just in time! 

    - James Daugherty 
     
    Has anything really touched your heart lately? 
    Two things have for me. I went to Oregon to 
    visit my dad who lives alone in a two-bedroom 
    house he owns with his dog Lucky. He’s eighty-
    one this year and is having health problems. He 
    has family visit him weekly and a cleaning lady 
    once a week. A nurse from Social Services stops 
    in as needed. 
     
    As we sat talking he reflected on some 
    homeless people we saw walking along River 
    Road. “How do they live?” he asked, reflecting 
    on his own good fortune. “No family, no home 
    of their own. How do th ey live?” As I looked 
    back over the years I realized how close my 
    wife and I are to the sa me future and a feeling 
    of understanding and gratitude for our own 
    good fortune permeated my emotions. 
     
    November 20
    th. I open the San Jose Mercury 
    News to the Valley section and my eyes catch 
    the story headline, “Died Homeless”. The story 
    is of the fifty three people who died homeless 
    this year in Santa Clara County (twenty nine 
    last year). These were victims of poverty, 
    illness, and unemployment. 
     
    The article continues to state that the 
    population is changing from low income and 
    unemployed to more people whoa re mentally 
    ill, disabled, and elderly, that are unable to care 
    for themselves. 
     
    In a valley that has made millionaires out of 
    people, and a few billionaires, in a country that 
    is a world leader in wealth as well as political 
    freedom, it seems oddly strange and out of 
    place that we cannot properly take care of the 
    least of these. Yet we can spend billions a day 
    to insure a people half way around the world, 
    freedoms and values most of us take for 
    granted. 
     
    In Santa Clara County, 20,000 people are 
    homeless per year and statewide the number is 
    over 360,000 annually. As Christmas 
    approaches followed by the new year, perhaps 
    some reflections are in order, and a little more 
    appreciation should be shown for what we have. 
    Perhaps, too, some sharing of that good fortune 
    with others. 

    The City of Sunnyvale is now accepting 
    applications to fill seven seats on the City’s 
    advisory boards and commissions. Boards and 
    commissions advise the City Council on policy 
    issues and are appointed by the Council, usually 
    for a term of four years.

    were elected to City Council seats in November, 
    creating three vacancies on that commission. 
    The Board of Building Code Appeals and the 
    Heritage Preservation Commission each have 
    two openings. Applications are currently being 
    accepted. 
     
    An information meeting fo r those interested in 
    serving is scheduled December 8 at 6 p.m. at 
    Sunnyvale City Hall, West Conference Room. 
    City Hall is located at 456 West Olive Avenue. 
    The application period for board and 
    commission appointments closes 5 p.m., Friday, 
    December 26, 2003. 
     
    For an application and more information, call 
    the Sunnyvale City Clerk’s office at  
    730-7595 or visit http://sunnyvale.ca.gov/ and 
    click on Boards and Commissions. 
