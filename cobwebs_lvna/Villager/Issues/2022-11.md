    - Recap of the October Meeting -
    For our October meeting, we invited Alpana Agarwal, the Founder and President of Helping Hands of Silicon
    Valley (HHSV). HHSV is a local non-profit of volunteers helping our local community. Starting in 2020, it’s a
    young organization, but it has already helped support our local unhoused residents, farmers in Monterey
    County, people displaced by the fires in Santa Cruz, and distributed hygiene kits to people in Sunnyvale. Below
    are some meeting highlights:
    ●HHSV provides hot meals (lunch, dessert, and snacks) to people on Saturdays and Sundays. The food is
    prepared fresh that morning, and recent offerings have ranged from simple pasta dishes to butter
    chicken.
    ●While there are more unhoused folks in North Sunnyvale than other parts of Sunnyvale, most of the
    unhoused people in Sunnyvale are from the Bay Area. In fact, the first people to set up in Lakewood
    Park are from the Lakewood and Fairwood neighborhood.
    
    ●If you see someone who is unhoused, please
    share HSSV’s contact information with them. You
    can also refer people to the Bill Wilson Center,
    which supports primarily young adults, LGBTQ+,
    and foster youth.
    ●HomeFirst is a large non-profit based in Santa
    Clara that works closely to support affordable
    housing. However, they have a limited number of
    case workers, so it’s hard for the case workers to
    build relationships with all of the unhoused
    residents. Sunnyvale Community Services is
    focused on preventing homeless and helping to
    keep people in their homes.HHSV tries to fill this
    gap by working in tandem with these larger
    non-profits.
    ●When a case worker first makes contact with an
    unhoused person, they try to understand what
    that person needs. Case workers use a VI-SPDAT
    questionnaire to document these needs. However,
    the questionnaire asks very personal things (e.g.,
    if someone has been a victim of a crime or been
    forced to do things against their will). Many
    unhoused people (like all of us) are hesitant to
    share vulnerabilities with a case worker, which
    means unhoused people often underreport their
    risks. As a result, the unhoused person scores
    lower on the VI-SPDAT and is eligible for fewer
    services than they should be. A copy of the
    VI-SPDAT can be found here:
    https://pehgc.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/VI-SPDAT-v2.01-Single-US-Fillable.pdf
    ●HHSV works to remove the barriers to help. They support people with things like food stamps,
    obtaining IDs, accessing medical care, and getting social security numbers. These things may seem
    trivial, but for someone who is unhoused (and has no address), it can be extremely difficult to access
    this support.
    ●Many people dislike staying in a shelter. A shelter is rows of beds with no personal space, so you must
    sleep next to strangers. There is a risk of people stealing or rummaging through your belongings.
    Shelters also often have curfews and restrictions (like no drug or alcohol use or no pets allowed).
    Additionally, many shelters are closed during the day, so residents have to pack up all their belongings
    and find somewhere to go until they are allowed back into the shelter in the evening. Because of the
    unideal environment, even if we had enough shelter beds for our entire unhoused population, it’s

    unlikely that all of our unhoused residents would choose to live in a shelter. Similarly, even designating a
    large parking lot as a place for people to pitch their tents is not ideal, since people would still be living
    in close proximity to strangers. Many unhoused people prefer to live in smaller communities where they
    know everyone. For safety, privacy, and more reasons, tiny houses or motel rooms are preferred housing
    options.
    ●Sunnyvale’s shelter is only available to unhoused people who have been referred.Therefore, an unhoused
    person cannot show up at the Sunnyvale shelter and get a bed. They must go through the assessment,
    be referred by someone, and there has to be a bed available for them).
    For more information on HHSV or to volunteer with them, visit their website
    https://www.helpinghandssv.org/about-us, email them at info@helpinghandssv.org, or call them at (408)
    663-1812.

    - Lakewood Park Tree Walk -
    Tree #5 (Purple Leaf Plum)
    In April, we highlighted Tree #1, the London Plane
    tree. From there, Tree #2 was the Italian Stone Pine,
    visible from the same spot as Tree #1. We then
    walked northeast, stopping at a concrete bench on
    your left for Tree #3, the Crape Myrtle. We continued
    our leisurely pace, arriving at Tree #4, the Saucer
    Magnolia, in August. After a rest for a few months,
    we are finally at the fifth tree on SUFA (Sunnyvale
    Urban Forest Advocates) Lakewood Park Tree Walk
    - the Purple Leaf Plum.
    Since you probably don’t remember where you were
    in August, start today at the corner of Lakechime
    Drive and Silverlake Dr. Look south (towards the
    playground). Still outside the fence is where you will
    gaze upon the Purple Leaf Plum, aka Prunus
    cersifera, aka the cherry plum tree.
    The Purple Leaf Plum grows up to 25 feet and is a deciduous tree native to Asia and Southeast
    Europe. Its leaves are dark red or purple, and the trees lose its leaves in early fall (usually by
    end of September). Its flowers - white or pale pink - emerge in early spring. The trees don’t
    produce fruit every year, but in years when they do produce fruit, the fruit comes in late summer.
    Good news! You can eat the dark purple fruit (yes, it’s a variety of plum) that the tree produces.
    The SUFA notes say the fruit is sour but works well in jams. For the more adventuresome, try
    soaking them in vodka and sugar for two weeks to create plum vodka.
    The trees need weekly watering when they are getting established, but once they have been
    established, they are somewhat drought resistant. However, they are susceptible to a lot of
    diseases and pests. Diseases like black not, leaf curl, fireblight, and powdery mildew will attack
    the trees, as will pests like aphids, Japanese beetles, and leafhoppers.
    Want to learn more? Check out these links:
    ●https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prunus_cerasifera

    Neighborhood Scavenger Hunt
    Welcome to the monthly scavenger hunt! Send your name, address (to confirm that you live in the
    Lakewood/Fairwood neighborhood), and answers to the three questions to vp1@lvnasv.org by
    November 30, and we will announce the winner and the correct answers in the next newsletter. If
    there is a tie for the winner, we will randomly select one person as the winner. The winner will get to
    choose a $10 gift card from World Wrapps, Wal-Mart, or Giovanni’s Pizzeria.

    What is the name of the organization that offers Tree Walks in different parts of Sunnyvale?
    a. Sunnyvale Urban Forest Advocates
    b. Sunnyvale Urban Forest Allies
    c. Sunnyvale United Forest Alliance
    2. True/False: Torrance Ave and Socorro Ave are both on the Lakewood side of the
    neighborhood..
    a. True.
    b. False.

    a. 3

    Hi neighbors,
    I recently learned that there is no FHUSD high school in
    Sunnyvale north of El Camino Real (ECR). The only high
    school north of ECR (Sunnyvale High School) closed in
    1981. I've joined a group of parents who are exploring the
    possibility of better supporting our high school students
    who live in North Sunnyvale. As a result, I'm looking to
    interview two groups of people. First, current high school
    students (or recent grads from high school) who can talk
    about their experience commuting to/from school,
    specifically what works well and what doesn't work well.
    Second, long-term residents who lived in Sunnyvale
    when the old high school was closed in 1981; I'd like to
    learn more about how the decision was made to close the
    school and what the community sentiment was at the
    time. If you fit in either of these groups (or are interested
    in helping to advocate for better opportunities for our
    high school students), please email me at
    cjbassoon@gmail.com
