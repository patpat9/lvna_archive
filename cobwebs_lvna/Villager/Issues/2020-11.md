    LVNA MEETING ON OCT 14
    Dr. Moshe Lewis, MD., joined us for a discussion on the ongoing 
    coronavirus pandemic and answered tons of great questions from you 
    and your neighbors. On top of being a practicing Bay-Area physician for 
    the past 15 years, Dr. Lewis also holds a master’s degree in Public Health. For those who weren’t able to join, we recorded the 
    discussion and highly recommend checking it out: https://youtu.be/mYSt-XS_67k
    A few takeaways:
    •	
    Is it absolutely necessary to wipe down products, including groceries and consumables, before they come inside the 
    house? No. The virus is largely spread through aerosols and, while it’s possible to contract it through contact with 
    contaminated objects, immune systems do a great job at fighting off small doses that enter our body so it’s a much lower 
    risk.
    •	
    Why do I hear that small children don’t need to wear masks but I see 2nd and 3rd graders wearing them? What’s the 
    difference? Docs have to be honest; there is a lot that we still don’t understand. This virus is breaking a lot of norms. 
    Even though the vast majority of children aren’t severely impacted by COVID-19, they can still spread it to others so it’s 
    important for them to wear masks as well to help prevent spreading it to others, particularly the older people they come 
    in contact with. If they see us wearing masks, they’ll do it too. We need to be good role models.
    •	
    Why are some schools opening and others aren’t? The response to this virus has been different all over the country 
    and the population/demographics of particular areas plays into those decisions. Areas with higher elderly populations 
    or where it’s more densely populated may take a more cautious approach whereas others may not. School districts are 
    following a similar approach. Without getting political, the lack of a consolidated response means that the response has 
    come from the local level so there’s a lot of inconsistency there.
    There’s so much more packed into this 1-hour discussion so, again, we encourage you to check out the video. Dr. Lewis does a 
    fantastic job at explaining this complex subject.

    FACTOID
    Bay-area rain is right around the corner! OK, no, that’s not our mind-blowing fact of the month. What is interesting, 
    though, is how that rain and our yard cleaning methods can have major consequences downstream (pun 
    somewhat intended).
    Lakewood Village streets, and really most streets for that matter, are designed with a convex profile so that water 
    naturally runs towards either side and gets carried into the storm drains, which then lead out into the bay. With our 
    current dry-spell still in gear, the leaves on our deciduous trees have been hanging on tight but are already starting 
    to give out and make their way to the ground. Wind and people are the primary causes of those leaves then 
    making their way into the street, followed by the storm drains. While leaves off our properties tend to fall into that 
    “out of sight; out of mind” category, their journey of destruction is just beginning.
    Leaves that are carried into storm drains can cause blockages, leading to flooded streets. A few years ago, 
    Fairwood was impacted pretty hard with flooding when Calabazas Creek maxed out, preventing the storm drains 
    from having a way to empty. Now that particular event wasn’t caused by too many leaves but a plugged storm 
    drain has the same result, whether it’s plugged by too much water at the end or by leaves blocking the path; roads 
    fill with water. This obviously leads to safety concerns for driving in knee-deep water and the potential for property 
    damage to our low-level houses when the wake makes its way up a driveway and into someone’s garage. But 
    perhaps the less obvious side effect is the overload of nutrients that those leaves carry.
    Leaves caught in a storm drain are in the optimal location to fast-track the decaying process. That decayed mass 
    of leaves is full of nutrients that basically act as a multi-vitamin for the bay. Those extra nutrients then lead to 
    algae blooms, choking out our local sea life. So, before you blow or rake those leaves into the street, consider an 
    alternative use for them which can actually help spruce up your garden next spring and preserve the balance of 
    our local wildlife. Check out last year’s November Villager for ideas, at lvnasv.org/villager.

    RECIPE CORNER
    Cashew Milk
    1.	 Soak cashews overnight. After they have been soaked, drain and 
    rinse them.
    2.	 Place cashews into a high-powered blender, along with water 
    and any other optional ingredients. Blend on high for 90-120 
    seconds or until liquid is smooth.
    3.	 Pour cashew milk into storage container and refrigerate. Enjoy!
    ratio: 
    1/4 CUP raw cashews (soaked overnight) to 1 CUP water
    splash of maple syrup, optional
    pinch of salt, optional (if adding sweetener)
    hint of vanilla extract, optional
    1 liter cashew milk = 1 cup raw cashews + 4 cups water
