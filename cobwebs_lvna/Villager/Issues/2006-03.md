    Help Wanted, or Opportunity Offered? 
    -Brian Smithson 
    Sometimes it seems like only last week that I read a 
    message from Craig Crawford, then president of the 
    LVNA, on the lakewoodvillage@topica.com mailing 
    list. But looking back, it seems like decades have 
    passed. In truth, it was about four years ago that I read 
    Craig's message, simply entitled "Help," in which he 
    said that the Villager editor had moved out of the area 
    and there was an urgent need for a new volunteer. 
    "The editor puts the articles in a newsletter format, 
    that's it, no more," it said. "Some of the requirements 
    are: Willing to spend 2 hours a month putting the 
    newsletter together. A small amount of computer skills. 
    A computer that has Microsoft Word." It sounded easy 
    enough and I was unemployed at the time, so I signed 
    up. I had no idea how much more would actually be 
    required, but more importantly, I had no idea how 
    much this job offered. I had plenty of computer 
    experience and a solid set of English skills, but no 
    experience with editing and publishing a newsletter, no 
    experience with the LVNA, and for a resident of more 
    than twenty years, surprisingly little knowledge of 
    Lakewood Village. All of that would change. 
    January 2002 was my first issue, and it looked just like 
    the previous month's issue. There was no time to get 
    creative, because my first deadline was five days after I 
    took the job, but it still took about eight hours to piece 
    it together. I thought that the newsletter needed a little 
    facelift, and so I gave the February issue a new look.

    During that year, I also began writing some of the 
    articles, started a "history project" to find out more 
    about Lakewood Village, and set up www.lvna.net and 
    e-mail addresses for the LVNA board. In subsequent 
    years, I tried different typefaces, added or removed clip 
    art, used more or fewer photos, and even tried an online 
    version of the newsletter for a month or two. 
    After two years of working solo, I was joined by Faith 
    Rogers as a backup editor, then co-editor, and now 
    THE editor. I'll help out if needed, but it's time for me 
    to move on and make room for some fresh ideas. The 
    Villager is in very capable hands. 
    By now, I know more than I ever imagined I'd know 
    about newsletters, editing, Lakewood Village, the 
    LVNA, and the joys and (mostly) sorrows of using 
    Microsoft Word. Some of these skills have been 
    directly beneficial in my career managing information 
    security projects: I write a monthly newsletter for my 
    company that is also translated into Japanese and 
    distributed to colleagues on three continents, and I am 
    the lead editor for a 400-page information security 
    standard that is being developed by a dozen companies 
    for the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers 
    (IEEE). Both, alas, are written in Microsoft Word. 
    But those are skills that I could have developed in 
    many other ways. I think that the real value of being an 
    LVNA Villager editor has come from meeting and 
    working with other LVNA volunteers and getting 
    involved in my community. From this experience, I 
    will always feel more connected to my home, 
    Lakewood Village. ■ 
     
     
    March in Your Yard and 
    Garden 
    •Sow seeds of summer vegetables 
    and annuals indoors. 
    •The bare-root season is almost over. 
    Buy soon and plant when the 
    weather is right for planting. 
    Complete those orders for bare-root 
    fruit trees from specialty nurseries, 
    which usually stop shipping bare-root fruit trees by the 
    end of March. 
    •Send in orders to seed companies before they run out 
    of stock later in spring. 
    •Wet weather is good for indoor gardening chores such 
    as cleaning and repairing garden tools. Do not forget to 
    sharpen the blades of pruning tools and shovels, and to 
    service the lawn mower. 
    •Begin weeding while the weeds are young and the 
    ground is still wet. 
    •The rice growers in the Sacramento Valley remove the 
    fibrous hulls from the grains. Rice hulls are an 
    excellent, inexpensive soil amendment. Contact a rice 
    growers’ cooperative to find the nearest local source. 
    •Fertilize roses, citrus, and all flowering plants that are 
    about to bloom. Trees, shrubs, and cool season lawns 
    could use a high-nitrogen fertilizer. 
    •Fertilize azaleas, camellias, and rhododendrons when 
    they finish blooming. 
    •Prune spring-blooming plants as they finish blooming. 
    Toward the end of the month (but before new growth 
    begins), prune back both oleanders and crape myrtles to 
    produce new flowering wood. 
    •Watch out for snails and earwigs. 
    •Use a strong spray from the hose to wash away aphids. 
    •After the danger of frost is past, prune hydrangeas, 
    fuchsias, and bougainvilleas. 
    •Plant out summer bulbs such as amaryllis, callas, 
    cannas, dahlias, gladiolus, lilies, and tuberous begonias. 
     
    These tips are from the book Northern California Gardening: A Monthby-Month Guide, by Katherine Grace Endicott, published by Chronicle 
    Books, San Francisco, copyright 1996, and are used with the publisher’s 
    permission. ■

    January

    paperwork was reviewed. Guest speakers for 
    upcoming general meetings were discussed. ■

    Interactive College and Career Practice Tests 
    Now Available Online 
    The Sunnyvale Library has added Learning Express 
    Library, a new subscription database, to the resources 
    available on its www.sunnyvalelibrary.org Web site. 
    Learning Express Library offers unlimited, aroundthe-clock access to skill-building interactive courses 
    in math, reading, and writing, as well as hundreds of 
    online interactive practice tests, including grade- 
    school achievement tests, SAT, GED, U.S. 
    citizenship, and civil service practice exams. 
    Complete explanations accompany each response so 
    test-takers understand why an answer is correct or 
    incorrect. 
     
    To access the Learning Express Library, visit the 
    library's Web site and click on Databases and Online 
    Resources. Select Learning Express Library from the 
    database list, and create your own user account by 
    entering your Sunnyvale Library card number. Library 
    cards are free and may be obtained from the library.

    Sunnyvale Had a Head Start on New 
    Hazardous Waste Rules 
    California’s newest hazardous waste rules banned as 
    of February 9 most consumer electronics, batteries, 
    and mercury-containing products from being placed in 
    the trash for disposal. But Sunnyvale has been giving 
    its residents and small businesses a head start: many 
    of these products can already be recycled right here in 
    town. 
    Virtually all electronic devices that contain a battery 
    or circuit board–including computers, printers, cell 
    phones, stereos, and even some appliances like 
    microwave ovens–are targeted in the new regulations, 
    because many of them contain heavy metals like lead, 
    copper, and chromium. When not disposed of 
    properly, these hazardous waste products can pollute 
    the environment. Household batteries, mercury 
    thermometers, thermostats with mercury, and 
    fluorescent tubes and compact bulbs are also among 
    the prohibited products. 
    Sunnyvale residents with curbside recycling can 
    recycle household batteries by placing them in a 
    heavy-duty, sealed plastic bag and setting it on top of 
    their recycling cart at curbside. Residents and small 
     
     
     
     
    businesses can also take fluorescent tubes, compact 
    bulbs, and household batteries to the Sunnyvale 
    Materials Recovery and Transfer (SMaRT) Station, 
    301 Carl Road, for recycling.  
    More information on hazardous waste recycling is 
    available by visiting www.sunnyvalerecycles.org or 
    calling (408) 730-7262, TDD (408) 730-7501. ■
