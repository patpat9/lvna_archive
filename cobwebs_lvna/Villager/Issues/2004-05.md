    Getting Sunnyvale’s History Out of 
    Storage 
    - Ursula Syrova, LVNA President 
     
    At the April general meeting of the Lakewood 
    Village 
    Neighborhood 
    Association 
    we 
    were 
    treated to an interesting presentation by Laura 
    Babcock, chair of the Sunnyvale Historical 
    Society and Museum Association.  Laura started 
    with a quiz about the city’s history and then told 
    us about the mission of this organization: to 
    build the Sunnyvale Heritage Park Museum.  
    The museum will be a look-alike reconstruction 
    of the original house of the Murphy family, 
    Sunnyvale’s founding family.  Originally located 
    in what became Murphy Park, the Murphy house 
    was lived in by members of the Murphy family 
    until 1953, when it was given to the city of 
    Sunnyvale.  The house was damaged by fire and 
    then later demolished, in 1961, to make way for 
    Central Expressway.  The reconstruction will be 
    a modern building meeting all public access 
    requirements, but it will retain some of the 
    layout of the original.  It will look just like the 
    original from the outside, but some of the 
    interior space has been redesigned to serve the 
    needs of the museum, which include an 
    extensive 
    hands-on 
    education 
    series 
    for 
    children.  Currently Sunnyvale’s third-graders 
    are able to participate in this “1905 House” 
    experience at one of the park buildings one time 
    during the year, but the new museum will 
    provide a much more convenient space that will 
    serve more children.  The children learn what 
    life was like in 1905 for the Murphy family, 
    complete with “a hands-on experience with 
    artifacts of the period, including kitchen tools, 
    washtubs and washboards, and old-fashioned 
    toys,” according to the Heritage Park Museum 
    website at www.heritageparkmuseum.org.   
     
    The Murphy Park building does currently house 
    a tiny Sunnyvale history museum, but most of 
    what could be displayed is stuck in storage.  The 
    new museum will finally allow all the wonderful 
    artifacts to come out and be seen.  The museum 
    will be located in Orchard Heritage Park, in the 
    area near the Community and Senior Centers at 
    El Camino Real and Remington, looking out on 
    the apricot orchard.  Children currently have the 
    opportunity to learn about apricot harvesting 
    and drying from Charlie Olsen, who runs the 
    apricot operation out of the barn in Orchard 
    Heritage Park, so the new museum will just add 
    to the rich history lessons available there.  This 
    project is a wonderful effort to preserve 
    Sunnyvale’s history, provide further education 
    about the early days in this area, and foster a 
    bit of local pride. 
     
    On April 17 a fund-raising walk around the 
    orchard added funds to the museum project, 
    and other fund-raising activities are planned for 
    the summer.  Much of the money needed to 
    begin construction of the museum has been 
    raised already, but a large amount is still 
    needed.  Consult the website if you are 
    interested 
    in 
    more 
    information, 
    at 
    www.heritageparkmuseum.org. 

    May 
    in 
    Your 
    Yard 
    and 
    Garden 
    ¾ Weed while the weeds are young 
    and the soil is still damp enough 
    to make pulling the weeds easy. 
    Do not let the weeds form seed 
    heads. 
    ¾ Add 
    young, 
    seed-head-free 
    weeds 
    to 
    the 
    compost 
    pile. 
    Remember to keep the pile as damp as a 
    wrung-out sponge for faster composting. 
    ¾ Watch out for snails and earwigs, both of 
    which devour seedlings. 
    ¾ Use a strong spray from the hose to wash 
    away aphids. 
    ¾ Wrap fruit tree trunks with a sticky tape to 
    control ants, but remember to wrap low so 
    that birds, particularly hummingbirds, will 
    not be attracted. 
    ¾ Prepare for the coming hot, dry season by 
    applying 
    a 
    mulch. 
    Rice 
    hulls 
    are 
    an 
    excellent, 
    inexpensive 
    soil 
    amendment. 
    Contact a rice growers’ cooperative to find 
    the nearest local source. 
    ¾ Prune spring-blooming plants as they finish 
    blooming. To keep old lilacs vigorous, prune 
    a few of the lilac stems to the ground each 
    year, and prune suckers off grafted varieties 
    of lilacs. 
    ¾ Fertilize lawns, roses, and all flowering 
    plants about to bloom. Fertilize azaleas, 
    camellias, and rhododendrons when they 
    finish blooming. If you did not fertilize them 
    last 
    month, 
    fertilize 
    citrus, 
    trees, 
    and 
    shrubs. 
    ¾ Plant out summer bulbs such as callas, 
    cannas, dahlias, gladiolus, lilies, tigridia, and 
    tuberous begonias. 
    ¾ Set out summer-flowering plants. 
    ¾ Plant 
    the 
    vegetable 
    garden 
    and 
    thin 
    vegetable seedlings that were sown too 
    close 
    together 
    by 
    snipping 
    them 
    with 
    scissors.

    Goats Return to Sunnyvale  
    - Sunnyvale press release 
    Like the swallows to San Juan Capistrano and 
    the buzzards to Hinckley, Ohio, the goats have 
    returned to Sunnyvale. 
     
    More than 600 goats, with about 160 sheep in 
    accompaniment, now blanket the landscape at 
    the closed Sunnyvale landfill. The animals, 
    complete with herding dogs, have been provided 
    by Natural Solutions, a Santa Cruz-based 
    business that rents out the goats clear the land. 
    Sunnyvale has had several fires over the years 
    at the landfill as the summer-dried grasses 
    become volatile. Ironically, in some instances 
    the fires have been ignited by the mowers being 
    used to cut the grass back for fire prevention. 
    Mechanical mowers also pose a threat to the gas 
    collection system which uses the methane gas 
    generated naturally by the landfill to power City 
    facilities. Damage in the past from mowers has 
    been costly to repair. The goats very effectively 
    reduce the fire hazard without any damage to 
    the gas collection system. 
     
    According to Scott Kulenguskey of Natural 
    Solutions, a mixture of South African Boer goats 
    and Spanish goats are working the hillside along 
    with South African Doper sheep. Four border 
    collies and a Great Pyrenees act as livestock 
    guards. 
    “Goats 
    provide 
    the 
    ultimate 
    in 
    ecologically-friendly 
    fire 
    prevention,” 
    said 
    Sunnyvale Solid Waste Program Manager Mark 
    Bowers. “The goats are far less damaging to our 
    gas collection system than mechanical mowers, 
    they reduce the spread of undesirable plant 
    species such as the yellow star thistle, and 
    goats don’t create noise or air pollution.” 
     
    Sunnyvale 
    pays 
    $20,000 
    for 
    the 
    goats. 
    Comparable costs for tractor mowing range 
    from $10,000 to $15,000. But in at least one 
    instance, 
    $12,000 
    worth 
    of 
    damage 
    was 
    attributed to the mowers. 

    Regulations, Shortage of Affordable 
    Housing Challenge Businesses in 
    Silicon Valley 
    UNSCC Advisory Council Hears Good and 
    Not-So-Good News 
    - Faith Rogers 
    A new wave of innovation and an upturn in the 
    economy will re-energize Silicon Valley in the 
    near future, the CEO and president of the San 
    Jose Silicon Valley Chamber of Commerce told 
    the 
    Advisory 
    Council 
    for 
    the 
    United 
    Neighborhoods of Santa Clara County (UNSCC). 
    Speaking at the March meeting of the Advisory 
    Council, Jim Cunneen also said that business 
    owners find state and local regulations, such as 
    the workers’ compensation rate and mandated 
    health insurance coverage, burdensome. Such 
    regulations, 
    Cunneen 
    said, 
    discourage 
    businesses from locating in the valley.  
     
    An Advisory Council member asked what could 
    be done about affordable housing if wages are 
    kept low. Cunneen said that some businesses 
    are taking housing costs into account by offering 
    higher starting pay. High-density housing near 
    transit centers is necessary and should be built, 
    he noted. 
     
    The Bay Area is chronically short of housing by 
    20,000 units, Beverly Bryant, executive director 
    of the Southern Division of the Homebuilders 
    Association of Northern California, told the 
    Advisory Council. She noted that this is an 
    important statistic because housing is crucial in 
    getting industry and business to come to this 
    area. High housing prices do keep companies 
    out. 
     
    Bryant said that upcoming state legislation will 
    propose that sales tax revenues go to the state 
    but property tax revenues go to the cities where 
    the taxpayers live. She also 
    said that “green” buildings, in which recycled 
    materials are used and which are energy 
    efficient, are needed because of dwindling 
    natural resources such as wood. 
    Few condos are built in the state, she noted, 
    because of the possibility of building-defects 
    lawsuits. A state bill in the works would give 
    make it mandatory for builders to have the 
    opportunity to repair defects before lawsuits are 
    filed.

    Sunnyvale Public Safety Expands 
    Volunteer Program 
    - Sunnyvale press release 
    The Sunnyvale City Council Tuesday (April 20) 
    unanimously approved the expansion of the 
    Volunteers in Public Safety (VIPS) program. 
    This innovative program uses volunteers in 
    low-risk enforcement and administrative roles 
    to enhance services provided to the community 
    and improve public understanding of the City’s 
    public safety mission. 
     
    The Sunnyvale Department of Public Safety 
    (DPS) has a strong history of using volunteers, 
    but 
    had 
    previously 
    limited 
    volunteer 
    assignments 
    to 
    administrative 
    duties 
    and 
    special events. The VIPS program, which the 
    Council approved as a pilot project in 2000, 
    marked the first time DPS had used volunteers 
    in enforcement activities. Volunteers in that 
    early program were assigned to perform 
    parking enforcement and abandoned vehicle 
    identification.  
     
    “Volunteers and volunteer programs are at the 
    heart of the City of Sunnyvale,” said Sunnyvale 
    Mayor John Howe. “By expanding this program, 
    we are going to be able to improve upon the 
    public safety services we already provide, while 
    being cost effective.” 
     
    Currently 10 volunteers, ranging in age from 
    college students to senior citizens, volunteer in 
    the VIPS program. They perform a wide range 
    of duties, from marking and tagging nuisance 
    vehicles for abatement to serving as public 
    safety chaplain. In total, volunteers give nearly 
    200 hours per month of their time to support 
    public safety. With its decision to continue the 
    VIPS program, the City Council also approved 
    an expansion of the program over the next two 
    years. This could add as many as 20 more 
    volunteers, and new services such as bike 
    licensing, traffic calming and neighborhood 
    watch coordination. 
     
    For more information about volunteering with 
    DPS, 
    including 
    current 
    opportunities 
    and 
    applications, visit the City of Sunnyvale Web 
    site at www.sunnyvale.ca.gov, and click on 
    Volunteering. You can also call (408) 7307533, 
    TDD 
    (408) 
    730-7501, 
    or 
    e-mail 
    volunteerresources@ci.sunnyvale.ca.us. 

    This Month in Lakewood History… 
    - Brian Smithson 
    45 years ago: In 1959, A “Lakewood Village 
    Aquatic Club” was announced. It was to 
    have been a members-only club at Manzano 
    and Oak Creek, with 2 adult pools, 1 child 
    pool, clubhouse, outdoor games area for 
    croquet and horseshoes, and plans for 
    miniature golf and tennis courts.  
    41 years ago: In 1963, the president of the 
    LVRA proclaimed the month of May to be 
    “Lakewood Beautiful” month. Every month 
    should be “Lakewood Beautiful” month. 
    37 years ago: Speaking of beauty, in May 1967 
    the first (and only) “Miss Lakewood Village” 
    beauty contest was held. Entrants were 
    judged for poise, talent, and beauty. No 
    formalwear was required, but the girls did 
    need to display themselves in one-piece 
    bathing suits. Times have changed… 
    33 years ago: At the other end of the beauty 
    scale, in May 1971, citizens of Lakewood 
    Village and surrounding areas successfully 
    mounted 
    a 
    campaign 
    to 
    stop 
    the 
    construction of a slaughterhouse located (I 
    believe) where there is now one of the 
    mobile home parks. 
    8 years ago: In 1996, Lakewood Village’s 
    Eugene 
    Goldsmith 
    was 
    among 
    five 
    Sunnyvale 
    residents 
    who 
    carried 
    the 
    Olympic Torch through Sunnyvale on its 
    way to the summer games in Atlanta.
