    Do you know the radon level in your home? Unfortunately, the answer 
    for most people is, “No.” Up until a few years ago, that would have 
    been my answer too. It wasn’t until seeing a post from someone on 
    Nextdoor, asking about neighbors’ radon levels, when I discovered it’s 
    something that everyone should be aware of.
    Radon is a naturally occurring gas, created when radium decays. 
    Radium can be found everywhere – in the soil, water, and rocks – so 
    it shouldn’t come as a surprise that radon is naturally found in the 
    atmosphere, all over the world.
    There’s no such thing as a “safe” level when it comes to radiation 
    exposure. However, the outside levels for radon are essentially 
    negligible and there’s not much you could reasonably do to improve 
    them anyway. The concern is for those of us fortunate enough to 
    live indoors. Radon is so small that it can work its way from the soil 
    and up through cracks in the foundation of buildings. Depending on 
    how porous the concrete slab is, it’s even capable of permeating the 
    concrete itself. You may recall from physics classes, way back in the day, that warm air rises. While especially 
    true in the winter, but even for many homes in the summer, inside temperatures tend to be higher than outside. 
    This essentially turns buildings into giant chimneys; pulling cooler air through cracks/pores from underneath the 
    structures as some of the hot air escapes through higher-elevation openings. That subterranean air may contain 
    radon, which then starts to build up inside the home/building. The half-life for radon is only about 3 days and 
    20 hours but that’s more than enough time to reach dangerously high levels, especially when there’s a constant 
    supply from underground. Considering most Lakewood Village homes still have their original 1950s slabs, likely 
    with plenty of cracks serving as vacuums for radon, there may be a steady supply coming into your home.  
    The EPA estimates that about 21,000 people die each year in the U.S. as a result of exposure to radon. It’s the #1 
    cause of lung cancer in non-smokers. In comparison, about 3,000 people die each year in the U.S. from house 
    fires. Building codes require smoke detectors throughout a home but most people completely overlook the

    If you don’t know the radon level in your home, you’re highly encouraged to test it. There are a few different 
    methods but the cheapest option is to purchase a charcoal test kit. These kits are typically under $20 and will 
    include the costs of shipping and lab fees. You simply place the kit in the lowest floor of your home (I don’t 
    think any of us have basements so that would likely be the main floor), wait the duration specified in the 
    instructions (typically 2-90 days), pack it back up, and mail it off to the lab. The lab will analyze it and inform you 
    of the results, via email. This is not intended to serve as a product recommendation, but here’s an example for 
    a complete radon test kit: https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00002N83E. Another option is to go digital. These 
    devices are similar in size to a small thermostat or a smoke detector and are typically mounted on a wall for 
    easy access and continuous readings. They’re more expensive than the one-time-use test kits, but allow for 
    both long-term and short-term readings. Also not intended to serve as a product recommendation, but here’s 
    an example: https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00H2VOSP8
    The EPA recommends radon mitigation efforts if your average radon levels are above 4.0 pCi/L. If they’re 
    between 2.0 - 4.0 pCi/L, the EPA suggests that you at least consider steps to reduce it. Outdoor levels are 
    in the ballpark of 0.3 pCi/L, and Congress set a long-term goal for all U.S. buildings to have indoor levels at 
    or below outdoor levels, through mitigation efforts and construction techniques. If you find that your levels 
    are above 2.0 pCi/L, don’t worry, they can be fixed! More on that in next month’s Villager. Spoiler alert – our 
    home was averaging around 9.7 pCi/L in the winter. Windows are typically open more in the summer so there’s 
    “fresh” (0.3 pCi/L) air circulation to help keep it down.

    RECIPE CORNER
    Caprese Panini
    2 slices bread
    pesto (couple spoonfuls)
    2-3 handfuls fresh spinach
    2 slices mozzarella
    tomato, sliced & seeds removed
    black pepper
    salt
    1/2 tbsp butter
    1 tbsp avocado oil
    1.	 In a medium skillet, add avocado oil and 2-3 handfuls of 
    fresh spinach. Cook for a couple of minutes or until the 
    spinach has wilted down. Set aside & let cool.
    2.	 Spread butter onto the outter-facing sides of your bread. 
    Spread pesto on the inner-facing sides of your bread.
    3.	 Using your hands or a clean kitchen towel, squeeze out as 
    much water as you can from the cooked spinach. Set aside.
    4.	 Assemble sandwich (suggested order listed): bread (pesto 
    side inward), cheese, cooked spinach, tomato slices, black 
    pepper & salt to taste, another layer of cheese, the other 
    slice of bread (pesto side inward).
    5.	 Cook over medium-low heat in a skillet or in a 
    panini press until the cheese has melted. Enjoy!
