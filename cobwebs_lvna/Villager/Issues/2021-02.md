    FACTOID
    After the recent downpours, followed by amazingly sunny days, this clover-imposter is starting to flourish. As 
    you drive through the neighborhood, you’ll likely notice the pretty yellow flowers… all over the place. In many 
    cases, a quick glance in the backyard may be sufficient to spot the endless clumps of green heart-shaped 
    foliage. Noticing a trend? Yeah, this stuff is everywhere!
    Commonly called “Bermuda Buttercup” (Oxalis pes-caprae), this Oxalis-family weed seems to love the Bay 
    Area. Once it takes hold, it’s a pain to get rid of. Native to South Africa, this invasive, noxious weed was 
    originally brought to California for its ornamental beauty – Whoops! The climate and soil here make excellent 
    growing conditions so eradicating it requires some serious persistence and tests of patience.

    OK, why is all of this relevant? Well, Lakewood Village is covered with it. The invasive nature of this plant will 
    completely smother out any other plants that can’t out-grow it. Unfortunately, that pretty much describes the 
    majority of our native species and all of the other pretty flowers we’d want in the garden. So, in addition to our 
    yards becoming a homogenized blend of yellow and green, the bulbs also find their ways into our streams, 
    wetlands, and parks, wreaking havoc on the native wildlife. Right now is the optimal time to dig them up 
    because they’re just starting to flower, indicating that they’ve exhausted their energy reserves. From now on, 
    their leaves will continue to soak up the rays and they’ll send all of that energy straight towards the growth of 
    new bulbils (next year’s plants). Each plant is capable of growing up to 20 of these new bulbils, translating to 20 
    new plants next year. You can see how this gets out of hand rather quickly.
    There’s a smidge of good news when it comes to their reproduction. Although they have flowers, they do not 
    reproduce via seed transmission here in the Bay. All new plants come from existing roots, bulbs, and bulbils 
    (next-year’s bulbs), i.e., completely underground. If it weren’t for birds and squirrels, their ability to spread 
    would be limited to just a few inches each year. As you’ve likely guessed, our neighborhood wildlife ensures 
    they have a way to go for distance. Aside from the wildlife, people are the predominant means for their spread 
    when they move dirt around their own yard, share with friends/family, or when we purchase new contaminated 
    plants from a nursery.
    What to do –
    •	
    If you have an area contaminate by Oxalis, do not spread the dirt anywhere else in the yard. Remember, the plant’s 
    natural ability to spread is only a couple inches each year so moving that dirt, if it contains bulbs/bulbils, increases the 
    potential coverage.
    •	
    Hand-digging is the most effective, albeit labor- and time-intensive, method for organic eradication. Using a hand 
    weeder or a shovel, dig below the bulb, lifting up the dirt while gently pulling on the plant. The goal is to get the plant 
    AND the bulb. It may take some practice and, even then, there will likely be many failed attempts to get the bulb. But 
    rest assured that each time you dig it up you’re chopping off its source of energy. If you keep it up, eventually it won’t 
    be able to make it back to the surface again – You win!
    •	
    Another option for at least thinning out the growth the following year is solar sterilization. This can be accomplished 
    during mid-summer by getting the soil wet and covering with clear UV-resistant plastic for about 6 weeks. Think of it 
    as a steam-cleaning for your garden. Note that this method will only kill the bulbs in the top few inches of the soil, and 
    that it’ll kill most other things living in that layer as well {insects, fungi, helpful microbes, seeds}.
    •	
    It’s a bit late this year, but covering the soil with a few layers of wet cardboard, followed by a few inches of compost or 
    mulch, in late fall, can smother them as they start to sprout. They’ll use all their energy attempting to reach sunlight 
    that they won’t be able to produce new bulbils that season.
    •	
    If all else fails, you may decide that chemicals are the only way to go. Remember to carefully read the instructions to 
    limit the amount that goes into the soil and to avoid too much damage to surrounding plants. Also, keep in mind that 
    these bulbs are resilient so it may still take years before you’ve completely eradicated it from your yard.

    RECIPE CORNER
    Pesto
    4 CUPS basil leaves, packed
    1 CUP parmesan, finely shredded
    3/4 CUP extra virgin olive oil
    1/2 CUP pine nuts
    1 TSP salt
    3 garlic cloves
    1.	 Add basil leaves, pine nuts, garlic, and salt to food 
    processor. Process until finely minced.
    2.	 With food processor running, stream in olive oil. Process 
    until you’ve reached desired texture.
    3.	 Add shredded parmesan and pulse with food processor to 
    combine.
    4.	 Taste and add additional salt, if needed. Enjoy!
