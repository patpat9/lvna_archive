    “Lucky” Recovering from Injuries 
    - Editor 
    Last December, a 10 month old Scottish terrier 
    named “Lucky” was set ablaze, allegedly by 
    neighborhood children. Surviving those injuries 
    was miraculous, but facing 
    of the high cost of treating 
    burns that extended over 
    15% of his body fo rced 
    Lucky’s owners to consider 
    putting him to sleep.  
     
    Fortunately, a veterinary technician where 
    Lucky received his initial treatment organized 
    donations to a fund to offset the costs. Those 
    donations, many from complete strangers, plus 
    reductions in cha rges from the veterinarians, 
    made it possible for Lucky’s owners to go 
    ahead with his treatment. Lucky is mostly 
    recovered and back home now, the kindness of 
    many making up for the cruelty of a few.  
     
    More pictures and story are at the veterinary’s 
    web site  www.sbvs.com/luckie_fund.htm and 
    in the January 15 th issue of The Sunnyvale 
    Sun. ¢

    I Remember 
    - Maxine Malstrom 
    On a hot day in August 1958, my young 
    husband and I stopped in Sunnyvale to look at 
    some Brandon -built homes. At the time, he 
    was studying E ngineering at San Jose State, 
    and was also working full time in Mountain 
    View as a technician. Our boys were three and 
    four years old and our daughter was due to be 
    born in a few months. I thought that the 
    houses were too small and they all looked the 
    same. However, my husband was excited that 
    the houses were only a minute from the 
    Bayshore highway and that the trip from work 
    and school would be shorter. He also pointed 
    out that with our limited income, the price was 
    right.  
     
    We paid the sales office $15.00  to hold a house 
    for us while we worked out a budget to be sure 
    we could afford the mortgage payments. The 
    next day, we gave them the $80.00 down (for 
    Veterans) and agreed to use the option of 
    buying a washer and dryer with the house. This 
    added extravagan ce caused our monthly 
    payment to be $101.00 per month. In those 
    days, you were told that your house payment 
    should never exceed ¼ of your monthly 
    income, and $101.00 was really pushing it. 
     
    The houses were all painted pale colors with 
    white trim and white rock roofs. The lawns 
    weren’t planted yet and there were no trees, 
    shrubs, or fences anywhere north of Sandia 
    Avenue. We learned to know our neighbors 
    quickly and shared our clever solutions to 
    common problems (like covering our windows 
    with glass wax unti l we could afford curtains or 
    drapes.) 
     
     
     
    There was no shopping center and most of us 
    were one-car families with Dad working and 
    u continued in next column  
    u continued from previous column  
    Mom at home. Going to the market required 
    walking several miles, and those of us who had 
    baby buggies were the lucky ones. I can 
    remember crossing Bayshore highway at the 
    signal (there was no overpass yet) and trying 
    to push the buggy full of groceries and hold my 
    little guys’ hands at the same time.  
     
    We had two dairies  within a mile our house and 
    they made a nice outing for my children and 
    me. Where 3Com is located, there was a dairy 
    that had big glass windows where you could 
    watch them milk the cows with machines. 
    Across what is now 237 was Coppla Dairy Farm 
    and they s till did most of their milking by hand. 
    Mountain View - Alviso Road  (now 237) and 
    Lawrence Station Road both were only two 
    lanes wide, and there were no signals on either 
    of them except at Bayshore. Traffic? Not much, 
    except for the trucks that hauled alo ng 
    Lawrence. Truck traffic was heavy because that 
    way was one of the shortest routes from the 
    east bay and the peninsula. Old Oakland Road 
    wasn’t old yet. 
     
    South of Bayshore on the Santa Clara side 
    were bean fields; east of the Village: Bartlett 
    Pear orcha rds; north: acres of tomatoes and 
    Swiss Chard. I still have volunteer chard 
    sprouting in my back yard, and it’s still better 
    than what you can buy in the market.  
     
     
     
    I’ll be moving from my home soon and it will 
    be with mixed feelings. I know I will miss my  
    house, my neighbors and my Village. Yet I am 
    looking forward to making a new home, new 
    friends and once again living near open fields 
    and orchards.

    Dear Editor, 
     
    It was with great concern that I read 
    Craig's threats to have vehicles 
    ticketed on sweeping days. I would like 
    to welcome him to the realities of 
    today. This morning I took the time to 
    count the cars, truck, trailers and RVs 
    along one block of Lakehaven. There are 
    about forty houses on this short bit of 
    road. There were 109 vehicles parked 
    outside, 34 of them on the street. One 
    house had four cars, four trucks (one a 
    duece-and-a-half) and a trailer, either 
    in the driveway, on the lawn or in front 
    of the house. Another has seven, three 
    others five each. I have a small RV and 
    will be adding three of four feet to 
    driveway so I can get my car into the 
    garage. 
     
    So, Craig, where do we park on sweeping 
    day? Your position might have made sense 
    when the Village was built,  but the days 
    of one car families is long gone. Are 
    you going to pave your yard and invite 
    us over? Aren't you the same person that 
    was upset by people paving their yards 
    or parking on their lawns?  
     
    There are two very simple things that 
    could be done to make sweeping possible: 
    alternate sides of the street sweeping 
    and starting the sweeping at a more 
    realistic time. Most of the "extra" 
    vehicles are gone by 7:30. I'm off to 
    work at 7:00. 
     
    By the way, there was a lot of wind 
    during that storm and many leaves blown 
    off of the City's trees. Not to mention 
    branches and a couple complete trees. 
    Maybe the time has come to cut the old 
    trees down, before they crush someone's 
    car, truck or child? It would also solve 
    the leaf problem, the clogged drain 
    problem and the trash on the sideways 
    problem.

    What’s So Special About the 
    LVNA? 
    - Brian Smithson 
     
    What's so special about the Lakewood Village 
    Neighborhood Ass ociation? For me, the LVNA 
    represents many things in our community: 
     
    Ø A group of people that any Lakewood 
    Village resident can talk to about any 
    neighborhood issues. 
     
    Ø A monthly meeting place where any and all 
    residents can hold group discussions.  
     
    Ø A clearinghouse and conduit of information 
    that is important to all residents.  
     
    Ø A loud and clear voice representing the 
    concerns of Lakewood Village to the City 
    and to the County.  
     
    u continued in next column  
    u continued from previous column  
     
    Ø An organizing force for social and 
    community events, like the parade, picnic, 
    dumpster weekends, etc.  
     
    Ø An opportunity for local volunteerism 
    though which people can “give back” to 
    their community, meet their neighbors, 
    learn and improve skills, and experience 
    the satisfaction of  contribution. 
     
    Ø A training ground for community leaders, 
    many of who have gone on to important 
    roles in the City and County government 
    (including our most recent Mayor!). 
     
    Ø An organization with many, many 
    accomplishments that make a difference to 
    residents every day. 
     
    Ø An outstanding example of self-
    determination and democracy at the 
    neighborhood level. 
     
    Ø A neighborhood tradition spanning forty-
    five years that is unique in Sunnyvale and 
    exemplary in Silicon Valley. 
     
    I think that it would be a big loss for Lake wood 
    Village if we did not have our neighborhood 
    association, but that is exactly what will 
    happen if people don't take the opportunity to 
    nominate themselves for office and fill the open 
    volunteer positions. 
     
    The LVNA (and it's predecessor, the LVRA) 
    have from time to time closed their doors 
    because of lack of participation. After some 
    time, people realize what was lost and how 
    much a neighborhood association is needed, 
    but it can take months or even years to regain 
    the momentum and organization necessaril y to 
    open it again. It is much easier to keep the 
    LVNA going than it is to start over again.  
     
    If you have an interest in your community and 
    a few hours to spare each month, I urge you to 
    consider the opportunities available in the 
    LVNA. For those of you wh o have participated 
    in the LVNA in the past, I invite you to come 
    back.  
     
    The LVNA, the meetings, the picnics, parades, 
    and other activities, and this newsletter, 
    cannot exist without volunteers like you. ¢

    Sunnyvale Completes Major 
    Bikeway Expansion 
    - Sunnyvale Press Release 
    Sunnyvale, CA (01/23/03) -- The City 
    of Sunnyvale has just completed the 
    construction of 11 miles of new on -street bike 
    lanes. This brings the total amount of bicycle 
    lanes in the City to 33, nearly doubling 
    Sunnyvale’s bikeway network within the last 
    three years. 
     
    The additional bike lanes are part of the City's 
    ongoing efforts to promote and encourage 
    bicycle travel and bicycle awareness. “The City 
    is becoming a more a ttractive place to bicycle 
    —  whether it is a mode of travel to work or 
    school, or for recreation” said Kevin Gregory, 
    Chair of the Sunnyvale Bicycle and Pedestrian 
    Advisory Committee. “We hope these additions 
    to Sunnyvale's network of bike lanes will 
    encourage those who live or work in the City to 
    try bicycling.”  
     
    The new bicycle lanes were designed and built 
    using mostly outside grant funding from the 
    Bay Area Air Quality Management District 
    Transportation Fund for Clean Air program, the 
    Federal Congestio n Management and Air 
    Quality program, and Transportation 
    Development Act Article 3 funds. Mayor Julia 
    Miller sits on the Board of Directors of the Bay 
    Area Air Quality Management District. 
    “Bicycling as a means to improve air quality is 
    greatly enhanced wi th the addition of these 
    bike lanes,” said Mayor Miller.  
     
    The projects are the result of a comprehensive 
    bikeway planning effort. The City's Bicycle 
    Capital Improvement Program, which was 
    adopted in 2000, prioritizes projects for 
    expanding the Sunnyvale b icycle lane network 
    and encouraging bicycle travel. Sunnyvale is 
    hoping to expand the bike lane network even 
    further, with bike lanes along Evelyn Avenue 
    under development.

    Sunnyvale Responds to 
    Proposed State Budget Cuts 
    Sunnyvale, CA (1/16/03) -- In 
    response to two specific budget 
    changes being recommended by the 
    State of California to reduce its deficit, the City 
    of Sunnyvale has outlined how this will impact 
    the City’s own budget and potentially affect its 
    services.  The Governor of California has 
    proposed transferring the Vehicle License Fee 
    from the cities to the state government, which 
    would eliminate $650 million to cities this year 
    and $1.45 billion for next year.  If this change 
    is implemented, Sunnyvale will lose over $8 
    million in vehicl e license fees this year and 
    next year. 
     
    Gov. Davis’ proposal also takes $75 million in 
    redevelopment authority funding allocated to 
    cities this fiscal year and an ongoing shift of 
    $250 million from cities beginning next year.  
    If this proposed change is i mplemented, 
    Sunnyvale could lose up to $2.5 million in 
    redevelopment funds over the next two years. 
    “We understand that the State has a very 
    difficult problem and that there needs to be 
    cuts,” said Sunnyvale Mayor, Julia Miller.  “But 
    we suggest that the S tate consider alternatives 
    to taking such deep cuts out of cities’ budgets 
    or we stand to have important city services
