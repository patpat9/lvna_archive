    City Budget Woes 
    - Fred Fowler 
     
    Greetings, Lakewood Village!  I wr ite to you 
    now because times have gotten very tough for 
    the City of Sunnyvale.  In a rather short time, 
    our economic fortunes have turned and we are 
    facing some grim choices.  Those choices will 
    affect every corner of our city.  I would like to 
    explain to you my understanding of what has 
    happened, how it happened, and what our 
    options are for moving on from here. 
     
    REVENUE AND EXPENSE 
     
    First, here are some basics about how the City 
    manages money.  City governments use a kind 
    of accounting called “Fund Accoun ting”, which 
    is different from the accounting methods used 
    by businesses.  In a business there are assets, 
    liabilities, revenues and expenses, all of which 
    are netted out to measure the profit or loss of 
    a business.  In government, there is no such 
    thing a s “profit” or “loss”.  There are only 
    sources of money are purposes for which 
    money is used.  Governments manage money 
    by matching up specific sources with specific 
    purposes they are supposed to serve.  These 
    “match-ups” are called “Funds”.  Thus there is 
    a fund called the Wastewater fund, which gets 
    money from sewer fees and uses it to run the 
    City’s sewer system.  Likewise there is the 
    Housing fund, which gets its money from 
    development fees and is used to increase the 
    supply of affordable housing.  There  are 21 
    major funds and numerous sub -funds 
    administered by the City. 
     
    There are basically two sources of money for 
    these funds:  fees and taxes.  Fees are used 
    when a “customer” can be identified who

    u continued from previous co lumn 
     
    agrees to use a specific service and is willing to 
    reimburse the City for the cost of providing it.  
    Taxes are used when no specific “customer” 
    can be identified ahead of time.  Our City -
    owned golf course charges a fee when 
    someone wants to play a ro und of golf: the 
    golfer is a specific customer who agrees to pay 
    the costs of his or her use of the course.  
    Conversely, taxes pay for our police and fire 
    services, as no one can predict ahead of time 
    whether he or she will be robbed or have their 
    house ca tch fire.  Also, no one wants to dicker 
    over the price when such things happen.  
     
    Our budget problems do not involve those City 
    activities that are funded through fees.  Our 
    City policy (generally) states that fees will be 
    set on a “cost recovery” basis -- as costs rise 
    and fall they are balanced by fee level 
    adjustments.  Our current problems involve 
    those funds that use tax money to do their 
    work.   
     
    There is a major tax based fund that is used to 
    pay for just about all of the “public benefit” 
    services pro vided by the City.  It is called the 
    “General” fund.  Our current problem can be

    summarized by saying that, due to a variety of 
    reasons, our General Fund currently has about 
    10% less tax reven ue than it needs.  Put 
    another way, we have recently found out that 
    our current plans to provide “public benefit” 
    services cost 10% more than the tax money 
    we have to pay for them.  Needless to say, 
    something has to change.  The City Council 
    and the commun ity have to figure out what to 
    do to balance the books.  We have to make 
    serious choices in order to bring our services 
    levels and our tax revenues back into balance.  
     
    HOW AND WHY 
     
    How did we get into this predicament?  That is 
    a good question.  Reasonable  people are 
    disagreeing about how it happened, and we 
    may never really know the entire story.  Still, 
    there are some broad outlines I will share with 
    you, along with my own personal opinion as to 
    what caused this mess.  
     
    We can all remember the great “dot.c om 
    boom” economy we had in the late 1990s, and 
    we are all living through the “dot.com bust” 
    now.  There is no question that the boom and 
    the bust affected our city’s finances.  From 
    1999 through the end of 2000 the City 
    collected far more tax money than it  ever 
    anticipated.  Since then, our revenues have 
    fallen back significantly.  Much has been made 
    of this, especially by the City Manager.  He lists 
    falling tax revenues as one of the three 
    primary causes of our current problems. 
     
    In this area, I disagree w ith the City Manager.  
    More tax money would definitely help, but I 
    don’t believe that falling revenues are the right 
    culprit to focus on in this case.  As evidence, I 
    will point out that we do long range forecasting  
     
    u continued on next column  
    u continued from previous column  
     
    of our revenue and expenses.  We routinely 
    predict how much money will be needed in 
    future years in order to provide a stable set of 
    services to our residents.  The budget we 
    adopted for the fiscal year 2000 -2001 
    predicted that we wou ld have about $95 million 
    dollars in tax revenue this year.  As it turns 
    out, we don’t have $95 million.   We have $97 
    million. 
     
    That’s right.  Even in this recession we have 
    more money now than we predicted we would 
    have only three years ago.  Something 
    changed drastically during those three years to 
    put us into the pickle we find ourselves in now.  
     
    SERVICE LEVELS AND COSTS 
     
    One thing that has NOT changed is the “level of 
    service” that the city is supposed to provide to 
    its residents and businesses.  Those  levels are 
    set by the City Council each year when the 
    budget is adopted.  Our policy is to maintain a 
    “status quo” for services and change them only 
    after a public debate.  There have been no 
    significant changes to our services in quite 
    some time.    
     
    What has changed is the cost to the city of 
    providing those services.  Since the 1999 -2000 
    budget year, our costs have risen almost 
    25%(!).  In other words, it now costs an 
    average of 25% more to do the same things 
    we were doing for 25% less just three years 
    ago.  Those costs have been growing at about 
    8% per year.  For the first two years, the 
    “dot.com boom” revenues hid the problem 
    from us.  Now that we are in the bust and our 
    revenues are at more “normal” levels, this cost 
    problem has hit us with a vengeanc e.

    WHAT TO DO? 
     
    The City Manager has proposed a “6 point 
    plan” to address the budget crisis.  That plan is 
    still unfolding, but its theme is to make 
    substantial cuts in just about everything t he 
    General Fund does in order to bring the 
    situation into balance.  So far, we have seen 
    proposed cuts in our list of “capital projects”.  
    In the near future we will see proposals to 
    forgo replacing old equipment, leave unfilled 
    positions vacant, cut servi ce levels and raise 
    taxes.  It will not be a pleasant set of choices 
    to make. 
     
    There are many projects and services near and 
    dear to Lakewood Village’s heart that will 
    probably have to be sacrificed to this problem.  
    High on that list is the Wildwood Inter section 
    project.  It is expensive and difficult and only 
    benefits Lakewood Village.  I don’t expect it to 
    be spared.  In my opinion, it fits the criteria I 
    would use to select projects for the chopping 
    block.  No one has fought for this project 
    harder than I have (I’ve spent 9 years!).  Even 
    so, it is probably in the best interests of the 
    city as a whole to use the money for other 
    things in this terrible budget situation we find 
    ourselves in. 
     
    In my opinion, there are two imperative needs 
    facing us.  First,  we have to come together as 
    a community in order to decide which General 
    Fund projects and services are the most 
    important ones to keep.  The City of Sunnyvale 
    doesn’t undertake any activity without a 
    purpose.  Everything we do with General Fund 
    tax reven ue has a good reason behind it and 
    usually directly benefits some or all of our city 
    residents.  We are at a point where we can’t do  
     
    u continued on next column  
    u continued from previous column  
     
    it all anymore.  We have to choose those 
    things that are the  most important to try to do.  
    After that, we will see how many of them we 
    can do.   
     
    Secondly, we must find out why our costs are 
    growing so fast, and we must bring that growth 
    under control.  Ultimately, our costs must not 
    grow faster than the revenues w e use to pay 
    for them.  As I said before, this growth is not 
    the result of a “spending binge” by the Council 
    on new projects and services.  It is the result 
    of having to pay more for the same service 
    levels that have been in place for many years.  
    In my opinion, it is a result of the way the City 
    has been managed for the last few years.  I 
    don’t know what specifically has gone wrong, 
    but I am certainly going to do my very best to 
    find out. 
     
    If we don’t make significant budget cuts now, 
    we will sacrifice the  present day life of our City.  
    If we do not control the growth of our costs, 
    we will sacrifice the future of our City.  The 
    present day budget cuts will be very painful.  If 
    we do not control the growth of our costs, no 
    amount of “present day” cuts will b e enough.   
    We will either control the growth or make more 
    painful cuts year after year until we do.  
     
    It isn’t a pretty picture.  Still, this is all about 
    choices.  The Council and the community have 
    to make choices about what things the City 
    should or shou ld not do.  We also have to 
    choose how to pay for the things we decide are 
    worth paying for.  The Council has a 
    responsibility to the community to make sure 
    that every tax (and fee) dollar is used 
    efficiently and effectively for the purpose 
    intended.  Ulti mately, the Council and the 
    community together have to decide what those 
    purposes should be. 
     
    I have great faith in our community.  
    Sunnyvale has attracted the most talented and 
    capable people in the world over the years.  
    Together we have more than enough  collective 
    wisdom to solve problems much worse that 
    this one.  All we have to do is to find a way to 
    work together on it.  I’ll be doing what I can to 
    make that happen.  I look forward to working 
    with you all. ¢

    This Month in Lakewood History… 
    - Brian Smithson 
    47 years ago: In 1956, General Motors 
    purchased 200 acres of land near 
    Lawrence and Oakmead. They decided 
    to build their auto manufacturing plant 
    in Fremont, otherwise it would have 
    been our neighbor! 
    45 years ago: In 1958, weekly street sweeping 
    was started.  
    44 years ago: In 1959, Lakewood School was 
    completed, and the Lakewood Shell gas 
    station opened for business.  
    40 years ago: In 1963, Fire Station #6 was 
    dedicated. 
    33 years ago: In 1970, Sunnyvale Library 
    Bookmobile service started.

    evening. He is a mix of a Yorkie and

    Sunnyvale Mayor 
    Appointed to Regional 
    Airport Planning 
    Committee 
     
    Sunnyvale, CA (02/06/03)  – Sunnyvale Mayor, 
    Julia Miller, was recently appointed to the 
    Regional Airport Planning Committee (RAPC), 
    comprised of representatives from the 
    Metropolitan Transportation Commission, the 
    Association of Bay Area Governments, the Bay 
    Conservation and Development Commission, 
    the region’s three international airports — San 
    Francisco, San Jose, and Oakland — and state 
    and federal agencies. 
     
    An important project that the RAPC is 
    undertaking is updating the Regi onal Airport 
    System Plan to inform and guide decisions 
    affecting runway capacity in the Bay Area over 
    the next 20 years.  “Since Sunnyvale is home 
    to Moffett Field, and the future use of this 
    airfield and its runways is extremely important 
    to the residents  of Sunnyvale and our 
    surrounding cities, it will be essential to have 
    this perspective represented,” said Mayor 
    Miller. 
      
     The Committee has also commissioned 
    new aviation forecasts for 2010 and 2020,
